\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:beer-introduction}

The preceding chapters have assembled, one by one, the ingredients needed to verify a translation: a set-theoretic universe (\Cref{ch:zflean}), a formal account of fragments of B and SMT-LIB with their respective semantics (\Cref{ch:b,ch:smt}), and a theory of representation changes between SMT types reconciling sets and types (\Cref{ch:loosening}).
This chapter puts them together.
It presents \beer{} (B Encoder), a tool written in Lean that translates Atelier~B proof obligations into SMT-LIB scripts, and---its actual subject---the formal soundness results currently established for this translation~\cite{trelat2025beer}.
The implementation is deliberately kept in the background: it is the topic of a single section (\Cref{sec:beer-implementation}), and everything before it is stated in ordinary mathematical language.

\subsection{What is proved}
\label{sec:beer-goal}

The correctness criterion for \beer{} is semantic equivalence: a B proof obligation and its SMT encoding must have the same truth value under corresponding valuations.
The Lean theorem proves the central semantic-preservation result for individual terms, including when the source and the target use different representations.
Under the hypotheses stated in \Cref{thm:beer-encodeTerm-spec}, a successful encoding of a well-typed, well-defined B term of type \(\alpha\) produces a well-typed SMT term of some selected representation type \(\beta\); its denotation represents the source denotation after the semantic cast from \(\beta\) to the canonical type \(\toSMT{\alpha}\).
The theorem applies directly to valuations that use the representations selected by the encoder, including option-valued functions.

At the level of arbitrary terms, however, semantic equivalence cannot generally be stated as literal equality of denotations.
The translation changes representations: a B set becomes an SMT characteristic predicate, while a relation known to be functional may be represented by an option-valued function, as developed in \Cref{ch:loosening}.
A B term \(t\) of type \(\alpha\) and its SMT encoding \(t'\) of type \(\beta\) are therefore generally denoted by elements of different carrier sets.
Their denotations must instead \emph{correspond} through the change of representation.

The kernel of the correctness result is consequently an agreement statement.
We first state it here informally, just to motivate the rest of the chapter.
For every well-typed, well-defined B term \(t : \alpha\) whose bounded universals satisfy the binder-compatibility condition of \Cref{sec:beer-soundness}, and for corresponding B and SMT valuations, a successful encoding produces an SMT term \(t' : \beta\) such that
\begin{equation*}
  \interpB{t} \semrel \interpS{t'},
  \label{eq:beer-invariant}
\end{equation*}
where \(\semrel\) is the agreement relation identifying an SMT denotation with the B denotation it represents.\footnote{The corresponding Unicode character \(\semrel\) is named \emph{corresponds to} (U+2258), which is its intended reading.}
The \emph{representation interval} \(\bigl[\lgf{(\toSMT{\alpha})},\mgf{(\toSMT{\alpha})}\bigr]\) of \Cref{ch:loosening} collects the SMT types at which a B term of type \(\alpha\) may be represented, ordered by loosening.
For Boolean terms it is the singleton \([\boolS,\boolS]\), and agreement reduces to equality of truth values; this specialization is the semantic fact used for proof obligations.

Both denotations are sets in the ZFC universe of \Cref{ch:zflean} augmented with typing information (the semantic triples) as developed in \Cref{ch:b,ch:smt}: B terms and SMT terms denote in one and the same model, so agreement is a genuine set-theoretic relation rather than transport between two unrelated semantics.
Agreement has two ingredients.
First, the target value must equal the source value once cast to the canonical type and read back through the retraction.
Second, a support condition requires that the members of a set-valued denotation themselves admit representatives in the target type; this is what makes the relation recursive.
This idea ruled out the possibility of a semantic interpretation of SMT-LIB in bare higher-order logic (HOL), even though it would have been a very natural choice.
This design decision, made in \Cref{ch:b,ch:smt}, is what this chapter relies on.
\Cref{sec:beer-semantic-bridge} constructs \(\semrel\); \Cref{sec:beer-encoding-rules} gives the encoding rules; \Cref{sec:beer-soundness} proves the main correctness statement; and \Cref{sec:beer-proof-obligations} derives the corresponding result for proof obligations.

\subsection{A running example}
\label{sec:beer-running-example}

The following small example accompanies the whole chapter.
We consider two sets of integers \(X, Y\) and two constants
\[
  \var{f} \inB X \tfunB Y,
  \qquad
  \var{g} \inB X \mathbin{\bop{\leftrightarrow}} Y,
\]
a total function and a relation, and state the invariant \(\var{f} \cupB \var{g} \subseteqB X \prodB Y\).
The B method would generate one proof obligation for it, whose goal is the invariant itself under the two hypotheses above:
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\var{f} \inB X \tfunB Y\bigr) \andB
  \bigl(\var{g} \inB X \mathbin{\bop{\leftrightarrow}} Y\bigr)
  \impB
  \var{f} \cupB \var{g} \subseteqB X \prodB Y.
  \label{eq:beer-running-po}
  \tag{$\star$}
\end{equation}
The example is small but not innocent.
After elaboration of the derived forms of \Cref{ch:b} (\(X \bop{\leftrightarrow} Y\) is \(\powB(X \prodB Y)\), and \(X \tfunB Y\) a comprehension over \(X \pfunB Y\)), the two operands of \(\cupB\) live at the same B type but use two different SMT representations: \(\var{f}\) is represented as an option-valued function, while \(\var{g}\) is represented as a characteristic predicate on pairs.
The total-function hypothesis is what justifies the functional representation of \(\var{f}\).
Their union consequently exercises the representation change of \Cref{ch:loosening}, while the inclusion goal---elaborated as a bounded universal quantification---exercises the binder rule.
Concretely, the selected SMT context contains
\[
  \Gamma(\var{f}) = \intS \toS \optS\,\intS,
  \qquad
  \Gamma(\var{g}) = (\intS \timesS \intS) \toS \boolS.
\]
If a source valuation assigns relations \(F\) and \(G\) to \(\var{f}\) and \(\var{g}\), respectively, the true total-function hypothesis makes the canonical graph of \(F\) functional.
The corresponding SMT valuation may therefore assign to \(\var{f}\) its functional collapse and to \(\var{g}\) its canonical characteristic predicate.
Turning the former back into a graph recovers the canonical graph of \(F\), so both assignments correspond to their source values despite inhabiting different SMT types.
The proof obligation \eqref{eq:beer-running-po} is worked through concretely in \Cref{sec:beer-rules-running-example}, revisited in the soundness argument of \Cref{sec:beer-soundness}, and shown as a complete emitted script in \Cref{sec:beer-proof-obligations}.
