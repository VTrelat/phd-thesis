\section{Results}
\label{sec:beer-results}

\subsection{Global picture}
\label{sec:beer-results-global}

\Cref{fig:eval-global} shows the strict outcome of every scored goal.
End to end, ppTrans proves \bmPptAll\,\% of the corpus against \beer{}'s \bmBeerAll\,\%---but the gap between them is not solely a solver result.
On \beer{}'s side, \bmNoScript\,\% of all goals never reached cvc5: the encoder failed or exhausted its budget on the enclosing file, or---in a small per-goal remainder---on the goal itself.
On ppTrans's side, \bmPptKilled\,\% of all goals were killed at the \(300\)\,s wall with the solver still running.
These two populations decide the comparison and are the principal exclusions under verdict-only scoring, one from each side.
They are not symmetric: of the \bmPOTotal{} goals ppTrans alone proves under strict scoring, roughly three quarters are goals for which \beer{} emitted no script---encoder \emph{coverage}, not encoding quality, is \beer{}'s binding constraint.

The two scorings tell different stories, and their combination is the first substantive result: under strict scoring, \beer{} is 17.9 points behind; restricted to the goals both pipelines actually answered, it is within 3.2 points (\bmBeerH\,\% against \bmPptH\,\%).
Both are true statements about different questions.
\Cref{fig:eval-fourway} splits that shared population four ways: \bmBoth\,\% of it is proved by both encodings, and each side proves goals the other cannot---\bmBeerOnly\,\% for \beer{} alone against \bmPptOnly\,\% for ppTrans alone.
The two encodings are thus far from nested: even under strict scoring, their union proves \bmSUnion\,\% of the corpus, almost six points beyond the best single pipeline, so a portfolio of the two is stronger than either.
Nevertheless, even where both scripts receive an answer, the higher-order encoding proves slightly fewer goals, and \bmNeither\,\% of the shared population resists both.
Higher-order SMT solving is a young capability~\cite{barbosa2019hosmt} and is not yet efficient for goals of this form and scale.

\Cref{fig:eval-matrix} crosses the two outcomes goal by goal over the goals that \beer{} encoded.
There, \emph{timeout} denotes the \(300\)\,s per-goal wall, whereas \smtinl{unknown} is the verdict that cvc5 returns at its own \(5\)\,s budget; verdict-only scoring retains exactly the top-left \(2\times2\) block.
Two of its cells matter beyond their size.
First, no goal is \smtinl{sat} on one side and \smtinl{unsat} on the other; indeed, cvc5 never returned \smtinl{sat} for any script.
A hard disagreement would indicate an unsound encoding or a misaligned join, so its absence across more than 1.6 million solver runs is a useful, if weak, empirical complement to \Cref{ch:beer}: the measured translator and its baseline, both unverified, at least never contradict each other.
Second, the \emph{timeout} row and column are highly asymmetric: \beer{} essentially never hits the wall, whereas ppTrans exhausts it on more than a tenth of the goals it shares with \beer{}---and on \bmBeerWinsPptKilled{} of those, \beer{} had already proved the goal in a median of about five seconds.

The time and size components of cost are reported in \Cref{fig:eval-time,fig:eval-ratio}.
On the \bmBothCount{} goals both pipelines prove, the two solving-time distributions track each other closely: \beer{} is faster on \bmBeerFaster\,\% of goals, and the median per-goal ratio is \bmRatioMed\(\times\), while the respective median solving times are \bmBeerMed{}\,ms and \bmPptMed{}\,ms.
Moreover, \(80\,\%\) of the per-goal ratios fall within a factor of about \(2\times\) either way.
The median script-size ratio is \bmSizeMed\(\times\) (\bmBeerSizeMed{}\,KB against \bmPptSizeMed{}\,KB)---the representation blow-up anticipated in \Cref{sec:beer-discussion}, with each cast contributing a helper variable and a quantified specification.
Aggregate solver cost tells the operational story more bluntly: running cvc5 over the corpus consumed roughly \(840\) hours on \beer{}'s scripts against \(9\,500\) hours on ppTrans's, with almost all of the difference spent at the \(300\)\,s wall on goals that produced no verdict.
For the \bmBeerWinsPptKilled{} goals that \beer{} proves while ppTrans reaches the wall, a shorter per-goal budget preserves \beer{}'s answer but yields no ppTrans verdict.

\Cref{fig:eval-order} cuts by type order.
The natural conjecture---that a higher-order encoding pulls ahead as types deepen but falls behind on flat goals---holds only in part.
Where both sides answer, the encodings perform comparably at order 0 and from order 2 upward; almost the entire verdict-only gap occurs at order 1 (\(-7.1\) points), where sets and relations are most prevalent in the corpus.
This restriction must also be considered: it removes a quarter of the order-0 goals but \(70\,\%\) of the order-\(\ge 4\) goals, precisely because deep goals are where \beer{} and ppTrans most often fail to produce a verdict (\(43.4\,\%\) and \(36.7\,\%\) of them, respectively), leaving 3\,921 of 12\,950 goals in the surviving deep bucket.
Under strict scoring, which counts all of those as failures, \beer{} is behind at every order; \Cref{tab:eval-order} carries both readings.

\Cref{fig:eval-subject,fig:eval-construct} locate the differences by subject and by construct, and they fall on either side of a clean line.
\beer{} is ahead wherever a goal involves \emph{counting}, \emph{integer arithmetic}, or a \emph{set treated as an object}: pure arithmetic (\(+9.0\)), sequences (\(+3.6\)), pure set theory (\(+1.5\)), and above all cardinality, where its success rate is nearly twice the baseline's (\(33.9\,\%\) against \(17.5\,\%\)).
It is behind wherever a \emph{function is applied}: the functions subject class (\(-9.6\)) and the function-application construct class (\(-7.4\)) carry the largest deficits, alongside transitive closure (\(-8.2\)).
The latter class must be read with care: only 634 of its goals survive the restriction because ppTrans was killed on \(45.8\,\%\) of the goals containing a closure.
Both signs are legible in the design of \Cref{ch:beer,ch:loosening}.
On the winning side, characteristic predicates keep the set algebra shallow---union is a pointwise disjunction, not an axiom about a fresh set---and integers stay native, so arithmetic reasoning never crosses an encoding layer.
On the losing side, every application of an encoded function passes through the option-valued machinery and, when representations disagree, through a cast whose helper variable and quantified specification help explain the blow-up measured in \Cref{fig:eval-ratio}; ppTrans's uniform membership relation has no such seam.
The propositional deficit (\(-5.7\)) fits neither pattern and is examined next.

\Cref{fig:eval-cross} crosses subject with the higher-order class of \Cref{sec:benchmark-corpus}, and its gradient provides the campaign's clearest evidence for the intended design.
In six of the seven subjects, \beer{} performs better relative to ppTrans on higher-order goals than on first-order goals of the same subject, and in four it is ahead outright---although the 70-percentage-point advantage in the higher-order arithmetic cell rests on only 161 goals and is too small a sample to support a general conclusion.
The characteristic predicate representation was adopted for exactly these goals---sets as objects, quantification over sets---and that is where it delivers; symmetrically, most of \beer{}'s remaining deficit sits in the first-order cells, where ppTrans's reduction is at home and \beer{} pays its representational overhead without collecting its dividend.

Finally, \Cref{fig:eval-size-quant} does not support two simpler explanations.
Neither raw goal size nor the number of quantifiers the \emph{source} goal binds separates the encodings: the gap ranges from 1 to 12 points across four orders of magnitude of size and from 3 to 7.3 points across the quantifier range.
The more relevant distinction is the quantified structure each encoding \emph{introduces}---casts and bounded universals on one side, membership axioms on the other.
These two axes do not capture that structure; the construct-based breakdowns reflect it more clearly.

\subsection{Where \beer{} wins alone}
\label{sec:beer-results-alone}

The preceding analysis begins from \beer{}'s overall deficit.
This section takes the opposite cut and examines the structure of the \bmBOHTotal{} goals that \beer{} proves and ppTrans does not.
Under verdict-only scoring, every such goal is one on which ppTrans answered \smtinl{unknown}: an encoding-quality win.
The strict picture is consistent: of the \bmBOTotal{} strict \beer{}-exclusive wins, \bmBOUnknown\,\% are goals ppTrans answered \smtinl{unknown} on, and only \bmBOKilled\,\% are resource wins at the wall.
The unit below is \emph{lift}: the ratio of the \beer{}-exclusive win rate within a class to the overall rate of \bmBOHRate\,\%, so a lift above 1 marks a class carrying more of those wins than its size would predict.
Lift says nothing about volume---a class can have a high rate and still contribute few goals---so the class sizes are tabulated alongside in \Cref{tab:eval-construct,tab:eval-subject}.

At the opposite end are function application (\(5.2\,\%\), a lift of 0.7) and, well below every other class, propositional goals (\(1.6\,\%\), a lift of 0.2).

\FloatBarrier

The wins are broadly spread but not uniformly: \bmBOFiles{} files contribute at least one exclusive win.
Three of the heaviest contributors\footnote{\texttt{0039/00060}, \texttt{0039/00040}, and \texttt{0032/00046}.} report identical figures and are almost certainly the same machine appearing three times in the corpus; they should therefore be treated as one when interpreting file-level concentration.
File \texttt{0033/00039} shows the exclusive wins are not confined to \beer{}'s favored classes: 2\,307 of its 3\,715 answered goals---\(62\,\%\) of the file, against ppTrans's \(35\,\%\)---are \beer{}'s alone, and they are order-1 function applications, the very class \beer{} loses on average.
A class-level deficit is an average, not a verdict on every goal in the class.
The files that this scoring \emph{removes} provide the converse lesson: on \texttt{0002/00006} and \texttt{0024/00011}, ppTrans proves barely \(2\,\%\) under strict scoring while \beer{} clears them in seconds, yet they cannot appear in a verdict-only ranking because almost none of their goals received a ppTrans verdict.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:beer-results-summary}

The campaign answers the four questions of \Cref{sec:evaluation-goals} as follows.
End to end, the \beer{} pipeline proves \bmBeerAll\,\% of the corpus against the industrial baseline's \bmPptAll\,\%.
The arithmetic of the gap is dominated by the \bmNoScript\,\% of goals that never reach the solver---mostly the \texttt{Struct} files that \beer{} does not aim to support---so encoder coverage, rather than encoding quality, is the principal end-to-end limitation.
Where both pipelines deliver a script and a verdict, the higher-order encoding stands within 3.2 points of a baseline refined over decades, with exclusive wins on both sides and a combined union of \bmSUnion\,\%, so the two encodings are complements rather than substitutes.
Structurally, the higher-order encoding performs best relative to the baseline where it was designed to excel: counting, arithmetic, and sets treated as objects, with a systematic relative advantage on higher-order goals; it fares worst on function application, where cast specifications concentrate.
Its cost profile is mixed: on goals that both pipelines prove, solving times remain comparable, but the median script-size ratio is \bmSizeMed\(\times\); end to end, ppTrans consumes far more aggregate solver time because it reaches the \(300\)\,s wall much more often.
