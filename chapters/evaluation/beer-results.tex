\section{Results}
\label{sec:beer-results}

\Cref{sec:beer-results-global} compares the two pipelines end to end and on the goals both answer, and reports the cost in solving time and script size; \Cref{sec:beer-results-where} cuts the comparison by goal class, \Cref{sec:beer-results-alone} examines the goals \beer{} proves alone, and \Cref{sec:beer-results-summary} returns to the questions of \Cref{sec:evaluation-goals}.

\subsection{Global picture}
\label{sec:beer-results-global}

\Cref{fig:eval-global} shows the strict outcome of every scored goal.
End to end, ppTrans proves \bmPptAll\,\% of the corpus against \beer{}'s \bmBeerAll\,\%.
On \beer{}'s side, \bmNoScript\,\% of all goals never reached cvc5: the encoder failed or exhausted its budget on the enclosing file, or---in a small per-goal remainder---on the goal itself.
On ppTrans's side, \bmPptKilled\,\% of all goals were killed at the \(300\)\,s wall with the solver still running.
Across more than 1.6 million solver runs, no goal is \smtinl{sat} on one side and \smtinl{unsat} on the other---the disagreement an unsound encoding would produce---and cvc5 never returned \smtinl{sat} for any script.

Under strict scoring, \beer{} is 17.9 points behind; restricted to the goals both pipelines actually answered, it is within 3.2 points (\bmBeerH\,\% against \bmPptH\,\%).
\Cref{fig:eval-fourway} splits that shared population four ways: \bmBoth\,\% of it is proved by both encodings, \bmBeerOnly\,\% by \beer{} alone, \bmPptOnly\,\% by ppTrans alone, and \bmNeither\,\% by neither.
Even under strict scoring, their union proves \bmSUnion\,\% of the corpus against ppTrans's \bmPptAll\,\%.
Those 3.2 points may simply be the price of a young technique: cvc5 is still the only SMT solver to accept the higher-order fragment of SMT-LIB~\cite{smtlib2.7}, and higher-order instantiation reached SMT solving only in 2019~\cite{barbosa2019hosmt}. Most other higher-order provers are saturation-based rather than SMT-based~\cite{bentkamp2023higherorder,kovacs2013vampire}, so \beer rests on solver support far less developed than the first-order reasoning ppTrans uses.

\Cref{fig:eval-matrix} crosses the two outcomes goal by goal over the goals that \beer{} encoded.
There, \emph{timeout} denotes the \(300\)\,s per-goal wall, whereas \smtinl{unknown} is the verdict that cvc5 returns at its own \(5\)\,s budget; verdict-only scoring retains exactly the top-left \(2\times2\) block.
The \emph{timeout} row and column are asymmetric: \beer{} essentially never hits the wall, whereas ppTrans exhausts it on more than a tenth of the goals it shares with \beer{}; on \bmBeerWinsPptKilled{} of those, \beer{} had already proved the goal in a median of about five seconds.

\Cref{fig:eval-time,fig:eval-ratio} report the two components of cost, solving time and script size.
On the \bmBothCount{} goals both pipelines prove, the two solving-time distributions track each other closely: \beer{} is faster on \bmBeerFaster\,\% of goals, the median \beer{}/ppTrans time ratio is \bmRatioMed\(\times\) in ppTrans's favor, and \(80\,\%\) of those ratios are within a factor of about \(2\times\) either way.
Taken separately, the median solving time is \bmBeerMed{}\,ms for \beer{} and \bmPptMed{}\,ms for ppTrans; these marginal medians do not divide to give the median ratio, which is computed goal by goal.
The median script-size ratio is \bmSizeMed\(\times\), \bmBeerSizeMed{}\,KB for \beer{} against \bmPptSizeMed{}\,KB for ppTrans---the representation blow-up anticipated in \Cref{sec:beer-discussion}, with each cast contributing a helper variable and a quantified specification.
Aggregate solver cost differs far more: running cvc5 over the corpus consumed roughly \(840\) hours on \beer{}'s scripts against \(9\,500\) hours on ppTrans's, with almost all of the difference spent at the \(300\)\,s wall on goals that produced no verdict.

\Cref{fig:eval-subject,fig:eval-construct} locate the differences by subject and by construct.
\beer{} is ahead where a goal counts, computes with integers (surprisingly), or treats a set as an object (which is the intended behavior): pure arithmetic, sequences, pure set theory, and above all cardinality, where its success rate is nearly twice the baseline's.
It is behind where a function is applied---the functions subject class and the function-application construct class carry the largest deficits---and on transitive closure, a class the restriction leaves with few goals because ppTrans was killed on nearly half of the goals containing a closure.
Both sides of that split follow the design of \Cref{ch:beer,ch:loosening}: characteristic predicates keep the set algebra shallow and integers native, whereas every application of an encoded function passes through the option-valued machinery and, when representations disagree, through the loosening phase.
Propositional goals follow neither pattern.
The rates behind both figures are in \Cref{tab:eval-subject,tab:eval-construct}.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:beer-results-summary}
The evaluation answers the four questions of \Cref{sec:evaluation-goals} as follows.
End to end, \beer{} discharges a smaller share of the corpus than the industrial baseline, and the goals that never reach the solver---mostly the \texttt{Struct} files it does not aim to support---account for most of that gap, so the principal end-to-end limitation is encoder coverage rather than encoding quality.
Where both pipelines deliver a script and a verdict, the higher-order encoding stands within a few points of the baseline, each side proves goals the other cannot, and their union proves more than either alone, so the two encodings are complements rather than substitutes.
Structurally, \beer{} gains on counting, arithmetic, and sets treated as objects, holds a relative advantage on higher-order goals in six subject classes of seven, and loses on function application, where loosening concentrates.
Its scripts are larger than ppTrans's and solve in comparable time on the goals both prove; end to end, ppTrans consumes far more solver time because it reaches the wall much more often.
Progress in higher-order instantiation would show up in \beer{}'s numbers and leave ppTrans's where they are: its scripts are first-order and already run on mature machinery.
