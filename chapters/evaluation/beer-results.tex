\section{Results}
\label{sec:beer-results}

\subsection{Global picture}
\label{sec:beer-results-global}

\Cref{fig:eval-global} shows the strict outcome of every goal slot.
End to end, ppTrans proves \bmPptAll\,\% of the corpus against \beer{}'s \bmBeerAll\,\%---but neither deficit is purely a solver result.
On \beer{}'s side, \bmNoScript\,\% of all goals never reached cvc5: the encoder failed or exhausted its budget on the enclosing file, or---in a small per-goal remainder---on the goal itself.
On ppTrans's side, \bmPptKilled\,\% of all goals were killed at the \(300\)\,s wall with the solver still running.
These two populations decide the comparison, and they are the ones the verdict-only scoring silently deletes, one from each side.
They are not symmetric: of the \bmPOTotal{} goals ppTrans alone proves under strict scoring, roughly three quarters are goals \beer{} emitted no script for---encoder \emph{coverage}, not encoding quality, is \beer{}'s binding constraint.

The two scorings tell different stories, and the pair of readings is the first substantive result: strictly, \beer{} is 17.9 points behind; restricted to the goals both pipelines actually answered, it is within 3.2 points (\bmBeerH\,\% against \bmPptH\,\%).
Both are true statements about different questions.
\Cref{fig:eval-fourway} splits that shared population four ways: \bmBoth\,\% of it is proved by both encodings, and each side proves goals the other cannot---\bmBeerOnly\,\% for \beer{} alone against \bmPptOnly\,\% for ppTrans alone.
The two encodings are thus far from nested: even under strict scoring, their union proves \bmSUnion\,\% of the corpus, almost six points beyond the best single pipeline, so a portfolio of the two is strictly stronger than either.
The sign of the residual gap deserves its own sentence: even where both scripts are answered, the higher-order one proves slightly fewer goals, and \bmNeither\,\% of the shared population resists both.
Higher-order SMT solving is a young capability~\cite{barbosa2019hosmt}, not yet efficient for goals of this shape---and size.

\Cref{fig:eval-matrix} crosses the two outcomes goal by goal, over the goals \beer{} encoded; \emph{timeout} there is the \(300\)\,s per-goal wall, distinct from the \smtinl{unknown} that cvc5 returns at its own \(5\)\,s budget, and the verdict-only scoring keeps exactly its top-left \(2\times2\) block.
Two of its cells matter beyond their size.
First, no goal is \smtinl{sat} on one side and \smtinl{unsat} on the other; indeed cvc5 answered \smtinl{sat} on no script at all.
A hard disagreement would mean an unsound encoding or a misaligned join, so its absence over 1.6 million solved scripts is a useful, if weak, empirical complement to \Cref{ch:beer}: the unverified translator and its unverified baseline at least never contradict each other.
Second, the \emph{timeout} row and column are wildly asymmetric: \beer{} essentially never hits the wall, while ppTrans burns it on more than a tenth of the goals it shares with \beer{}---and on \bmBeerWinsPptKilled{} of those, \beer{} had already proved the goal, in a median of about five seconds.

Time and size, the two cost questions, are settled by \Cref{fig:eval-time,fig:eval-ratio}.
On the \bmBothCount{} goals both pipelines prove, the two solving-time distributions track each other closely: \beer{} is faster on \bmBeerFaster\,\% of goals, the median per-goal ratio is \bmRatioMed\(\times\) (medians of \bmBeerMed{} against \bmPptMed{}\,ms), and \(80\,\%\) of the ratios fall within a factor of about \(2\times\) either way.
The higher-order scripts are larger, by \bmSizeMed\(\times\) in the median (\bmBeerSizeMed{} against \bmPptSizeMed\,KB)---the representation blow-up anticipated in \Cref{sec:beer-discussion}, each cast contributing a helper variable and a quantified specification.
Aggregate solver cost tells the operational story more bluntly: discharging the corpus took cvc5 roughly \(840\) hours on \beer{}'s scripts against \(9\,500\) hours on ppTrans's, almost all of the difference burned at the \(300\)\,s wall on goals that produced no verdict.
A user without a generous per-goal wall gets \beer{}'s answers, or ppTrans's silence, on that tenth of the corpus.

\label{sec:beer-results-where}

\Cref{fig:eval-order} cuts by type order.
The natural conjecture---that a higher-order encoding pulls ahead as types deepen and behind on flat goals---survives only in part.
Where both sides answer, the encodings perform comparably at order 0 and from order 2 upward; the entire verdict-only gap occurs at order 1 (\(-7.1\) points), where sets-and-relations are most prevalent in the corpus.
The restriction must be read alongside: it removes a quarter of the order-0 goals but \(70\,\%\) of the order-\(\ge 4\) ones, precisely because deep goals are where \beer{} and ppTrans give up (\(43.4\,\%\) and \(36.7\,\%\) of them, respectively), leaving 3\,921 of 12\,950 goals in the surviving deep bucket.
Under strict scoring, which counts all of those as failures, \beer{} is behind at every order; \Cref{tab:eval-order} carries both readings.

\Cref{fig:eval-subject,fig:eval-construct} locate the differences by subject and by construct, and they fall on either side of a clean line.
\beer{} is ahead wherever the difficulty of a goal is a \emph{counting}, an \emph{integer}, or a \emph{set treated as an object}: pure arithmetic (\(+9.0\)), sequences (\(+3.6\)), pure set theory (\(+1.5\)), and above all cardinality, where its advantage is nearly a factor of two (\(33.9\,\%\) against \(17.5\,\%\)).
It is behind wherever a \emph{function is applied}: the functions subject class (\(-9.6\)) and the function-application construct class (\(-7.4\)) carry the largest deficits, with transitive closure (\(-8.2\)) beside them---a class to read with care, since only 634 of its goals survive the restriction, ppTrans having been killed on \(45.8\,\%\) of the goals containing a closure.
Both signs are legible in the design of \Cref{ch:beer,ch:loosening}.
On the winning side, characteristic predicates keep the set algebra shallow---union is a pointwise disjunction, not an axiom about a fresh set---and integers stay native, so arithmetic reasoning never crosses an encoding layer.
On the losing side, every application of an encoded function passes through the option-valued machinery and, when representations disagree, through a cast whose helper variable and quantified specification are precisely the blow-up measured in \Cref{fig:eval-ratio}; ppTrans's uniform membership relation has no such seam.
The propositional deficit (\(-5.7\)) fits neither pattern and is examined next.

\Cref{fig:eval-cross} crosses subject with the higher-order class of \Cref{sec:benchmark-corpus}, and its gradient is the sharpest design validation in the campaign.
On six of the seven subjects, \beer{} does relatively better on the higher-order goals than on the first-order ones of the same subject, and on four it is ahead outright there---though the \(+70\) of the higher-order arithmetic cell rests on 161 goals and is not quotable.
The characteristic predicate representation was adopted for exactly these goals---sets as objects, quantification over sets---and that is where it delivers; symmetrically, most of \beer{}'s remaining deficit sits in the first-order cells, where ppTrans's reduction is at home and \beer{} pays its representational overhead without collecting its dividend.

Finally, \Cref{fig:eval-size-quant} rules out the two lazy explanations.
Neither raw goal size nor the number of quantifiers the \emph{source} goal binds separates the encodings: the gap is 2.8 points on the smallest goals, stays between 1 and 12 points across four orders of magnitude of size, and between 3 and 7.3 points across the quantifier range.
What the solver struggles with is the quantified structure each encoding \emph{introduces}---casts and bounded universals on one side, membership axioms on the other---which is invisible in these two axes and entirely visible in the construct cuts above.

\subsection{Where \beer{} wins alone}
\label{sec:beer-results-alone}

Everything above measures a deficit.
This section takes the opposite cut: the \bmBOHTotal{} goals \beer{} proves and ppTrans does not, and asks what they are made of.
Verdict-only throughout, which means every one of these is a goal ppTrans answered \smtinl{unknown} on: an encoding-quality win.
The strict picture is consistent: of the \bmBOTotal{} strict \beer{}-exclusive wins, \bmBOUnknown\,\% are goals ppTrans answered \smtinl{unknown} on, and only \bmBOKilled\,\% are resource wins at the wall.
The unit below is \emph{lift}: the rate of \beer{}-exclusive wins inside a class over the overall rate of \bmBOHRate\,\%, so a lift above 1 marks a class carrying more of those wins than its size would predict.
Lift says nothing about volume---a class can have a high rate and still contribute few goals---so the class sizes are tabulated alongside in \Cref{tab:eval-construct,tab:eval-subject}.

At the other ends sit function application (\(5.2\,\%\), lift 0.7) and, well below anything else, propositional goals (\(1.6\,\%\), lift 0.2).

\FloatBarrier

The wins are broadly spread but not uniformly: \bmBOFiles{} files contribute at least one.
Three of the heaviest contributors\footnote{\texttt{0039/00060}, \texttt{0039/00040}, and \texttt{0032/00046}.} report identical figures and are near-certainly the same machine appearing three times in the corpus, to be counted once.
File \texttt{0033/00039} shows the exclusive wins are not confined to \beer{}'s favored classes: 2\,307 of its 3\,715 answered goals---\(62\,\%\) of the file, against ppTrans's \(35\,\%\)---are \beer{}'s alone, and they are order-1 function applications, the very class \beer{} loses on average.
A class-level deficit is an average, not a verdict on every goal in the class.
And the files this scoring \emph{removes} are the mirror lesson: on \texttt{0002/00006} and \texttt{0024/00011}, ppTrans strictly proves barely \(2\,\%\) while \beer{} clears them in seconds, yet they cannot appear in a verdict-only ranking at all, because almost none of their goals got a ppTrans verdict to compare against.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:beer-results-summary}

The campaign answers the four questions of \Cref{sec:evaluation-goals} as follows.
End to end, the pipeline proves \bmBeerAll\,\% of the corpus against the industrial baseline's \bmPptAll\,\%.
The arithmetic of the gap is dominated by the \bmNoScript\,\% of goals that never reach the solver---mostly the \texttt{Struct} files \beer{} does not aim to support---but its root cause is the solver: higher-order SMT solving is not yet efficient for this kind of industrial problems.
Where both pipelines deliver a script and a verdict, the higher-order encoding stands within 3.2 points of a baseline refined over decades, with exclusive wins on both sides and a combined union of \bmSUnion\,\%, so the two encodings are complements rather than substitutes.
Structurally, the higher-order encoding is ahead precisely where its design aimed: counting, arithmetic, and sets treated as objects, with a systematic relative advantage on higher-order goals; it pays for this on function application, where cast specifications concentrate.
