\section{Benchmark corpus}
\label{sec:benchmark-corpus}

The corpus is the publicly available dataset of B proof obligations released by David D\'eharbe~\cite{deharbe2022pog}: \texttt{.pog} files generated by Atelier~B from industrial developments.
It contains 5\,421 files grouped into 40 anonymized project directories, one file per proved component.
Each file carries a list of proof obligations, each containing one or more \emph{goals}; the goal is the unit of measurement throughout this chapter.
The campaign scores \bmGoals{} goals from \bmFiles{} files; \Cref{sec:experimental-setup} delimits this population.
This is the largest B proof-obligation benchmark on public record, and its scale matters: several of the effects reported below live in classes of tens of thousands of goals, invisible at the scale of a hand-picked sample.

Beyond raw scale, the analysis needs a vocabulary for \emph{what kind} of goal each one is.
Every goal is classified along four structural axes, computed from its abstract syntax tree.

\paragraph*{Type order.}
The \emph{order} of a goal is the deepest nesting of \(\powB\) in the types of its subterms: order~0 goals mention only scalars, order~1 goals manipulate sets and relations---a relation being a set of pairs---and order~2 and beyond involve sets of sets, as arise from powersets, set-valued functions, or quantification over subsets.
The corpus is dominated by order 1 and 2 goals---around \(80\,\%\)---where only \(12\,950\) goals reach order \(\ge 4\).

\paragraph*{Subject.}
Seven mutually exclusive classes describe what a goal is about: \emph{sequences}, \emph{arith+sets} (integer arithmetic mixed with set or relational structure), pure \emph{arithmetic}, \emph{functions}, the remaining \emph{relational} algebra (composition, restriction, products, closure), pure \emph{set theory} (membership and inclusion only), and \emph{propositional} goals.
Function application is deliberately split from the relational algebra: it is the construct \beer{} encodes by an option-valued function rather than by a graph, and it behaves unlike the rest of the relational fragment.
The relational fragment in the broad sense---the \emph{relational} and \emph{functions} classes together---covers \(64\,\%\) of the corpus, as expected of a language whose data model is relational.

\paragraph*{Constructs.}
A complementary, overlapping classification asks how each side fares \emph{among the goals containing} a given construct: cardinality, transitive closure, function application, a relation-typed subterm, an explicit binder (\emph{quantified} covers \(\forallB\), \(\existsB\), \(\lambdaB\), \(\Sigma\), \(\Pi\), generalized union and intersection, and set comprehensions, which behave alike here), or a bound variable that is itself a set.
Only \bmQuantified{} goals bind a variable of their own; the quantifiers the solver wrestles with are overwhelmingly the ones each encoding \emph{introduces}.

\paragraph*{Higher order.}
A goal is \emph{higher-order} when its order is at least 2 or it quantifies over a set---the two situations the characteristic predicate representation of \Cref{ch:beer} is designed for.
This is a property of the B goal, not of either encoding: it marks where a set is treated as an object, whatever the target representation.
Slightly more than half of the corpus (\bmHOn{} goals) is higher-order in this sense, so the design decision under test is exercised by no marginal class.

Goal sizes span four orders of magnitude, from a handful of AST nodes to tens of thousands.
One caveat travels with the corpus: it contains near-duplicates---at least one component appears three times verbatim under different projects---which is accounted for in \Cref{sec:threats-to-validity}.
