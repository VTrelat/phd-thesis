\section{Generating the Lean theorems}
\label{sec:barrel-lean-metaprogramming}

An obligation read from a proof obligation file has already been assembled into a single
implication; what remains is to translate it into a Lean proposition, to supply the
well-definedness evidence that \Cref{sec:barrel-wd} makes mandatory, to attempt a proof,
and to contain the failures.

\subsection{Flattening and dependent conjunction}
\label{subsec:barrel-normalization}

Two normalizations are applied before elaboration.

The first is cosmetic.
A proof obligation arrives with its hypotheses bundled into conjunctions, and an implication
whose antecedent is an \(n\)-ary conjunction is rewritten into a chain of implications,
\[
  (H_1 \andB \dots \andB H_n) \impB G
  \quad\rightsquigarrow\quad
  H_1 \impB \dots \impB H_n \impB G .
\]
The two are equivalent, but the second form yields a Lean goal whose hypotheses are
separate binders, so a user proof does not begin with a sequence of administrative
conjunction eliminations.

The second is not.
A B conjunction is translated by a \emph{dependent} conjunction,
\[
  P \mathbin{\land'} Q \;\triangleq\; \exists\, (h : P),\ Q\,h ,
\]
and an implication by a genuine dependent \(\forall\).
The reason is that the well-definedness evidence required by the right conjunct may only be
derivable from the left one.
The B assertion
\[
  f \inB A \pfunB B \andB f(\var{x}) \inB B
\]
is a case in point: the term denoting \(f(\var{x})\) needs a proof that \(f\) is functional,
which follows from the first conjunct and from nothing else in sight.
With a non-dependent conjunction the second component would have to be translated in a
context where the first is unavailable, and the encoding would not even be well typed.
Translating the right-hand side under a hypothesis naming the left-hand side removes the
problem entirely, and does so uniformly for conjunction, implication, and the guard of a
lambda abstraction.

\(\land'\) is Lean's existential quantifier, given an infix notation and a matching
pretty-printer.
Writing the existential as such would have been equally correct and strictly less readable,
since every B conjunction would then appear as \(\exists\,(h : P),\ Q\,h\) in the
generated goals, and a reader comparing a goal with its B source would have to undo the
encoding at each connective.
When \(Q\) does not use \(h\) the binder is suppressed, and the goal shows
\(P \mathbin{\land'} Q\), one prime away from the B predicate it translates.

\subsection{Reifying well-definedness holes}
\label{subsec:barrel-reification}

The encoder must produce well-definedness evidence while it is still building the term
that needs it, and at that moment the evidence does not exist.
It therefore leaves a hole.
A hole of this kind---a \emph{metavariable}---carries two things: the proposition that must
eventually fill it, and the variables and hypotheses that were in scope where it was
created, which is by construction the context of the occurrence.
Once the enclosing proposition has been built, each remaining hole is \emph{reified}: its
proposition is universally closed over that context and declared as a theorem in its own
right, and the hole is filled by that theorem applied to the very hypotheses under which
the partial operator occurred.

\Cref{fig:barrel-reification} follows the mechanism on the obligation
\[
  \forallB \var{S} \inB \mathsf{FIN}_1(\ZintB) \cdot
    \bigl( (\forallB \var{x} \inB \var{S} \cdot \var{x} \leB 0) \impB \minB(\var{S}) \leB 0 \bigr),
\]
whose only occurrence of a partial operator is the minimum.

Nested partial operators are handled in the same way: a hole opened while translating the
argument of another partial operator has the larger context.
A proposition that still contains an unfilled hole is rejected rather than kept.
The main proposition and each of its reified conditions are named after the tag Atelier~B
gave the obligation, of the shape \(\mathit{Machine}.\mathit{Tag}\_i\) and
\(\mathit{Machine}.\mathit{Tag}\_i.\mathtt{wd}\_j\); a proposition becomes a declaration in
the environment as soon as it has a proof, carrying a docstring that records the source
artifact and the reason string of the obligation.

A principal proposition mentions the constants naming its definedness conditions, so those
conditions must be declared first, and the discharger therefore processes the reified
conditions of an obligation before the obligation itself.
What it fails to prove is collected in two groups---the artifact's open definedness
conditions, then its open principal obligations---and that is the order in which
\leaninl{prove_obligations_of} asks for proofs, a detail the user meets again in
\Cref{sec:barrel-interface}.

\subsection{Sharing well-definedness conditions}
\label{subsec:barrel-wd-sharing}

Reifying every occurrence separately is faithful, but it amplifies a duplication that is
already present upstream.
When Atelier~B splits a conjunctive invariant, it emits one obligation per conjunct while
repeating the common hypotheses; every partial expression inside those hypotheses is then
encountered again in each obligation, and the same well-definedness proposition would be
declared---and proved---many times.

The discharger therefore keeps, for the duration of an import, the well-definedness
theorems it has already created, and identifies a new condition with an old one up to
\(\alpha\)-equivalence, falling back on definitional equality.
When a match is found, the hole is filled with the existing theorem instead of creating a
new goal.

The mechanism has three properties.
It spans the whole import, and it covers conditions that were left open as well as those
already proved, so an interactive proof is never requested twice for the same condition.
It is equality-based, not subsumption-based: \barrel{} does not attempt to discover that
one condition follows from a differently stated, stronger theorem, which would add an
open-ended proof-search problem to goal generation.
Finally, it preserves a simple dependency structure, since every occurrence of a partial
operator still points to one theorem of exactly the type its operator demands.
The quantitative effect is reported in \Cref{subsec:barrel-minsearch-results}: on the case
study it removes more than half of the generated goals, and leaves the principal
obligations untouched.

\subsection{Proof-producing automation and the trust boundary}
\label{subsec:barrel-automation}

Before anything is shown to the user, the discharger runs the tactic
\leaninl{barrel_solve} on every distinct proposition---well-definedness conditions and
principal obligations alike.
The tactic is a small ordered cascade: it strips leading binders, tries \leaninl{trivial},
then the B-specific well-definedness tactics, and finally Lean's \leaninl{grind} procedure.
The well-definedness tactics are backtracking searches over lemma collections identified by
label attributes---\leaninl{wd_min}, \leaninl{wd_max}, \leaninl{wd_app} and
\leaninl{wd_card}, with two auxiliary collections \leaninl{pfun} and \leaninl{tfun} for the
function-space side conditions that those searches generate.
The collections cover four of the five partial operators; \(\mathsf{size}\), which the
case study does not exercise, has none, and its conditions fall through to
\leaninl{grind}.
\Cref{lst:barrel-automation-lemmas} shows representative members of the first collection,
together with a computation lemma of the kind that lets \leaninl{grind} make progress on a
goal once its definedness bookkeeping has been discharged.

\begin{leanlisting}[title={Representative lemmas driving \barrel{}'s automation.},
    label={lst:barrel-automation-lemmas}, float=t]
@[grind ., wd_min]
theorem min.WD_singleton {α} [PartialOrder α] {a : α} : min.WD {a}

@[wd_min]
theorem min.WD_of_finite {α} [LinearOrder α] {S A : Set α}
    (h : S ∈ FIN₁ A) : min.WD S

@[grind ., wd_min]
theorem min.WD_interval {lo hi : ℤ} (h : lo ≤ hi) :
    min.WD (lo .. hi)

@[grind ., simp]
theorem interval.min_eq {lo hi : ℤ} (h : lo ≤ hi) :
    min (lo .. hi) (min.WD_interval h) = lo
\end{leanlisting}

The tagged tactics remove the bookkeeping introduced by explicit proof arguments, which is
the part of a goal that no general-purpose tactic can be expected to handle, since it is
specific to the encoding.
Once that layer is gone, an obligation is a statement about sets, relations, orders, and
integers, and general automation applies.
Users may invoke the same tactics manually, mix them with standard Lean tactics, and add
their own lemmas to the label collections, so a development can extend the default
reasoning without replacing the discharger.

Each translation and each automatic attempt runs under its own resource budget rather than
a share of one budget for the whole import, and exhausting a budget is a local failure: the
obligation joins the list left to the user and processing continues.
Encoding errors are contained in the same way---an obligation that uses a construct outside
the supported fragment is reported as a warning and skipped, while its neighbors are still
attempted.
Without this containment a single divergent tactic call would abort the import of the
whole file, and industrial files would be out of reach
(\Cref{subsec:barrel-industrial-scale}).

The automation layer is proof-producing: a successful attempt installs only a term that
Lean's kernel accepts, and a failed or timed-out attempt leaves an ordinary interactive
goal.
One qualification applies: a goal that already depends on an admission is closed by
re-admitting it rather than rejected, and the discharger counts such outcomes separately
from genuine proofs; the
``auto-solved'' figure reported by the interface is the sum of the two, and the admitted
ones are rendered distinctly.

That guarantee stops at the propositions \barrel{} emits, and
\Cref{fig:barrel-trust} makes the boundary explicit.
Everything to the left of it is trusted: Atelier~B's generation of the obligation for a
source import, the reader that parses the resulting file, the encoder that turns B syntax
into Lean terms, and the faithfulness of the shallow model of \Cref{sec:barrel-embedding}
to B's semantics.
Everything to the right is checked: a completed, admission-free script yields a proof term
verified by Lean's kernel, and neither the tactics nor the interface of
\Cref{sec:barrel-interface} has to be trusted for that verification to hold.
Importing an existing proof-obligation file removes the external programs from a particular
run, but says nothing about how that file was produced.
\Cref{subsec:barrel-verified-generator} takes up the trusted side.

Within this boundary, two guarantees hold.
The first is structural: a partial B operator cannot occur in a translated term without
explicit well-definedness evidence, whether that evidence is produced during import or by a
user's tactic script.
The second is proof-theoretic: every theorem accepted without an admission has a checked
proof of its encoded statement, and its axiom dependencies can be listed.
