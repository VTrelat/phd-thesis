\section{A relational calculus in ZFC}
\label{sec:zflean-rel}

Throughout this section, we work within the ZFC model provided by Mathlib and extend it with a calculus for binary relations and partial/total functions.
A \emph{binary relation} $R$ between $A$ and $B$ is a subset $R \subseteq A\times B$.
Our calculus integrates standard operations on relations (converse, identity, composition, domain, range, image) together with predicates for partial and total functions, and provides \emph{automation tactics} designed to discharge ubiquitous well-formedness side-conditions, enabling these objects to be manipulated directly without verbose boilerplate, much as in a paper proof.
These tactics are designated \inlinelean|zrel|, \inlinelean|zpfun|, and \inlinelean|zfun|, and are described in greater detail in \Cref{subsec:zflean-automation}.

\subsection{Basic definitions}

\begin{definition}[converse, identity, composition][ZFSet.Id, ZFSet.inv, ZFSet.composition][ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.Id, ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.inv, ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.composition]
  \label{def:rel-basic}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The \emph{identity relation} $\Id{A}$ on a set $A$ is defined as:
          \begin{equation*}
            \Id{A} \;\triangleq\; \{ x \in A \times A \mid \pi_1(x) = \pi_2(x) \}
          \end{equation*}
          The identity relation is shown to be a total function belonging to $A^A$, and later shown to be a bijection and the neutral element for composition.
    \item Given a relation $R\subseteq A\times B$, its \emph{converse} $R^{-1}$ is defined as:
          \begin{equation*}
            R^{-1} \;\triangleq\; \{z \in B\times A \mid (\pi_2(z), \pi_1(z))\in R \}
          \end{equation*}
          This definition carries the side-condition $R\subseteq A\times B$ (declaring that $R$ is a relation between the implicit sets $A$ and $B$), an implicit argument that \inlinelean|zrel| attempts to discharge in \zflean.
    \item Given relations $R\subseteq A\times B$ and $S\subseteq B\times C$, their \emph{composition} $S\circ R \subseteq A\times C$ is defined as:
          \begin{equation*}
            S \circ R \;\triangleq\; \{ w \in A\times C \mid \exists\, y \in B,\, (\pi_1(w), y)\in R \land (y, \pi_2(w))\in S \}
          \end{equation*}
          Unlike the converse, the composition carries \emph{no} side-condition on $R$ and $S$: it is defined for arbitrary sets and, being a separation of $A\times C$, always yields a relation between $A$ and $C$.
          When $R$ and $S$ are genuine relations between $A,B$ and $B,C$, it computes their expected composition; any pairs of $R$ or $S$ lying outside $A\times B$ or $B\times C$ are simply ignored.  
          For functions specifically, we define a dedicated \emph{functional composition} $\zbinop{\circ}$ that requires $R$ and $S$ to be functions, and uses the \inlinelean|zfun| tactic to discharge these side-conditions automatically.
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Domain, range, image][ZFSet.Dom, ZFSet.Range, ZFSet.Image][ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.Dom, ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.Range, ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.Image]
  \label{def:dom-range-image}
          For a relation $R\subseteq A\times B$, its \emph{domain}, \emph{range}, and \emph{image} of a set $X\subseteq A$ are defined as:
  \begin{align*}
      \Dom(R)      & \;\triangleq\;  \{ x\in A \mid \exists\, y\in B,\, (x,y)\in R \}          \\
      \Ran(R)      & \;\triangleq\;  \{ y \in B \mid \exists\, x \in \Dom(R),\, (x,y) \in R \} \\
      \Image{R}{X} & \;\triangleq\;  \{ y \in B \mid \exists\, x \in X,\, (x, y) \in R \}
  \end{align*}

  Operationally, all three definitions above invoke the \inlinelean|zrel| tactic to discharge the side-condition that $R$ is a relation between $A$ and $B$.
\end{definition}

Finally, we define partial functions as functional relations without totality requirements.
\begin{definition}[Partial functions][ZFSet.IsPFunc][ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.IsPFunc]
  A set $f$ is said to be a partial function from set $A$ to set $B$, written $\mathsf{IsPFunc}(f,A,B)$\footnotemark, if it is \emph{functional}:
  \begin{equation*}
    \regnotation{\IsPFunc}(f, A, B) \;\triangleq\; f \subseteq A \times B \,\land\, \left(\forall\, x \in A,\, \forall\, y \in B,\, \forall\, z \in B,\, (x,y) \in f \land (x,z) \in f \implies y = z\right)
  \end{equation*}
\end{definition}
\footnotetext{The order of arguments is voluntarily changed compared to $\IsFunc$, so that in Lean, one may write \inlinelean|f.IsPFunc A B|.}

We also derive predicates $\IsInjective$, $\IsSurjective$, and $\IsBijective$ in the usual sense, which all invoke the tactic \inlinelean|zfun| to discharge functional totality requirements.

\begin{remark}
  These predicates are therefore stated for \emph{total} functions. Although they could be stated for partial functions, we enforce totality by design. The motivation is practical: in the case of a partial function, one is forced to state injectivity and surjectivity on the function's domain directly (partial functions are total on their domain).
\end{remark}
  
\subsection{Function evaluation and λ-abstraction}
\label{subsec:zflean-func-eval-lambda}

We now add two notions that make functions practical to use, namely function application and $\lambda$-abstraction.
In a purely set-theoretic setting these are not primitive---a function \emph{is} its graph, and application is the derived operation that recovers the unique image of a point---yet they are indispensable in practice; we define them so as to keep standard mathematical notation while automating side-conditions as much as possible:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{function application}, written $\fapply{f}{x}$, maps $x\in\Dom(f)$ to the unique $y$ such that $(x,y)\in f$; here $\fapplysym$ is the application operator (not a function applied to a pair), and it invokes the \inlinelean|zpfun| tactic to prove that $f$ is a partial function.
  In full, $\fapplysym$ is a non-computable dependently typed operator that \emph{also} takes a proof $h_x$ that $x$ belongs to the domain (supplied explicitly---there is no automation at this point), so the complete form is $\fapply{f}{\langle x, h_x \rangle}$, of which $\fapply{f}{x}$ is the shorthand we use throughout the manuscript; the membership proofs are elided here but present in the Lean code.
  Its definition uses Lean's choice operator to select the element $y$ with $(x,y)\in f$; since $f$ is functional this $y$ is unique, so this is really a \emph{definite description} rather than a genuine appeal to the axiom of choice.
  
  Another equivalent alternative would be to define $\fapply{f}{x}$ as the set \(\bigcup \setcomp{y \in B}{(x,y) \in f}\), which is also non-computable. We prefer the first, more direct formulation.
  Since $f$ is functional, there is at most one such $y$, and since $x$ belongs to the domain of $f$, there is at least one such $y$, hence such a $y$ exists and is unique---this property is proved in \zflean{} as the theorem {\small\leancodeptr{IsPFunc.exists\_unique\_of\_mem\_dom}{ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.IsPFunc.exists_unique_of_mem_dom}}.
  
  We show that evaluating a composite reduces to composing the evaluations, that is,
  \[
    \fapply{(g \zbinop{\circ} f)}{x}=\fapply{g}{\fapply{f}{x}}, \tag{\small\leancodeptr{fapply\_composition}{ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.fapply_composition}}
  \]
  and that $\fapply{f}{x}$ coincides with the unique element of the image $f[\{x\}]$,
  \[
    \fapply{f}{x} = \bigcap f[\{x\}] \tag{\small\leancodeptr{fapply\_eq\_Image\_singleton}{ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.fapply_eq_Image_singleton}}.
  \]

  \item $\lambda$-abstraction constructs functions from expressions, written as:
    \begin{equation*}
      \left(\zlambda{A}{B}{x}{e(x)}\right)
      \qquad\text{is the relation}\qquad
      \{ (x,y)\in A\times B \mid y=e(x) \},
    \end{equation*}
\end{itemize}
which is then shown to belong to $B^A$ whenever $e(x)\in B$ for all $x\in A$.

Function application being \emph{partial}, $\fapply{f}{\langle x, h_x \rangle}$ is defined only when $f$ is a partial function and $x$ lies in its domain, both of which must be witnessed.
A common alternative is to make every function \emph{total}.
This could be achieved by returning ``junk values''---some fixed default values---outside the intended domain; this approach is sometimes taken in Mathlib, usually indicated by appending "\leaninl{D}"---default---to the identifier's name.
We deliberately avoid this approach: a junk value would make $\fapply{f}{x}$ denote something even outside the domain, where the function is mathematically undefined---a technical device for reconciling partial mathematical functions with the totality required in dependent type theory, rather than a faithful set-theoretic notion.
Staying close to the set-theoretic principles, we instead adopt a \emph{by-construction} discipline: objects are formed together with proofs that they are meaningful.

\begin{remark}
  \label{rmk:no-junk}
  The price of this choice is the proof obligations that decorate our operators; the automation layer described in \Cref{subsec:zflean-automation} is precisely what keeps this price hidden in practice, discharging these obligations structurally so that the user writes the mathematics rather than the bookkeeping.
\end{remark}

The definitions above are stated at the mathematical level.
In \zflean, $\lambda$-abstraction bodies are in fact written with a \emph{dependent} conditional:
\begin{leanlisting}
λᶻ : A → B
   | x ↦ if h : x ∈ A then e(x) else ∅
\end{leanlisting}
The sole purpose of the test \leaninl{if h : x ∈ A} is to bring the membership proof $h$ into scope, since it is often required, \eg to form the application $\fapply{f}{\langle x, h \rangle}$: the bare binder $x \mapsto \cdots$ does not record that $x\in A$, even though the abstraction ranges over $A$.
The $\mathsf{else}$ branch is therefore dead code---never selected, because the specification of $\zop{\lambda}$ already restricts membership to $A$---and the placeholder $\varnothing$ is immaterial.
An alternative that avoids the conditional altogether is to let the abstraction bind over the subtype \leaninl|{x : ZFSet // x ∈ A}|, so that the membership proof is recovered by destructing the bound variable; we keep the present form for uniformity with the rest of the calculus.
  
We prove the expected equational properties for this notation, summarized in the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Basic properties][lambda\_spec, lambda\_ext\_iff, fapply\_lambda, lambda\_eta][ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.lambda_spec, ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.lambda_ext_iff, ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.fapply_lambda, ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.lambda_eta]
  \label{thm:lambda}
  Let $A$, $B$ be sets, $f \in B^A$, and $e,e' \colon \mathsf{ZFSet} \to \mathsf{ZFSet}$ be Lean-level (meta)functions on sets.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Specification:} For any sets $x$ and $y$, the following holds:
          \begin{equation*}
            (x, y) \in \left(\zlambda{A}{B}{x}{e(x)}\right) \iff x \in A \land y \in B \land y = e(x)
          \end{equation*}
    \item \textbf{Extensionality:}
          \begin{equation*}
            \left(\zlambda{A}{B}{x}{e(x)}\right) = \left(\zlambda{A}{B}{x}{e'(x)}\right) \iff \forall\, x \in A,\, e(x) = e'(x)
          \end{equation*}
    \item \textbf{$\beta$-reduction:}
          \begin{equation*}
            \forall\, x \in A, \fapply{\left(\zlambda{A}{B}{x}{e(x)}\right)}{\langle x, \irrelprf \rangle} = e(x)
          \end{equation*}
    \item \textbf{$\eta$-expansion:}
          \begin{equation*}
            f = \left(\zlambda{A}{B}{x}{\fapply{f}{\langle x, \irrelprf \rangle}}\right)
          \end{equation*}
  \end{itemize}

  As mentioned above, this mathematical presentation elides some implementation details;
  in particular, \(\eta\)-expansion is stated as follows:
  \begin{leanlisting}
f = (λᶻ : A → B
        | x ↦ if h : x ∈ A then @ᶻf ⟨x, $\irrelprf$⟩ else ∅)
  \end{leanlisting}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Proofs are carried out by applying extensionality on the corresponding sets.
\end{proof}

Application and abstraction integrate well with rewriting, so that many proofs reduce to algebra on images, evaluation, and composition, as expected.
We illustrate this with a simple example below.

\begin{example}
  \label{ex:func-ext}
  Consider the extensionality principle for total functions: two functions $f,g\in B^A$ are equal if they are pointwise equal on $A$.
  In \zflean, this is stated as the following theorem {\small\leancodeptr{is\_func\_ext\_iff}{ZFLean/Functions.html\#ZFSet.is_func_ext_iff}}:
  \begin{leanlisting}
theorem is_func_ext_iff {A B : ZFSet} {f g : ZFSet}
  (hf : IsFunc A B f) (hg : IsFunc A B g) :
    f = g ↔ ∀ x, (hx : x ∈ A) → @ᶻf ⟨x, $\irrelprf$⟩ = @ᶻg ⟨x, $\irrelprf$⟩
  \end{leanlisting}
  The hypothesis $h_x : x\in A$ is precisely what discharges the two omitted domain-membership obligations, namely $x\in\Dom(f)$ and $x\in\Dom(g)$, after rewriting $\Dom(f)=\Dom(g)=A$.
  The proof of this theorem can be carried out directly using extensionality on sets, although it turns out to be rather tedious.
  It is in fact easier to $\eta$-expand both $f$ and $g$ and apply extensionality from \Cref{thm:lambda} on the resulting $\lambda$-abstractions.
  In \zflean, this amounts to the following rewriting steps:
  \begin{leanlisting}
  rw [lambda_eta hf, lambda_eta hg, lambda_ext_iff]
  \end{leanlisting}
  This ends up in lifting a proof of extensionality for ZFC functions to standard extensionality of Lean functions.
\end{example}

\subsection{Automation of side conditions}
\label{subsec:zflean-automation}
These side conditions are not inherent to set-theoretic reasoning; they are the cost of the by-construction discipline mentioned in \Cref{rmk:no-junk}, which trades junk values for well-formedness witnesses in order to keep every object well-formed without threading hypotheses through definitions.
Working with relations and partial/total functions then repeatedly produces such subtype-shaped goals.
As already mentioned, the library provides three lightweight tactics whose purpose is to try to solve them \emph{structurally}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \inlinelean|zrel|: relational goals $R\subseteq A\times B$;
  \item \inlinelean|zpfun|: partial functionality goals $\mathsf{IsPFunc}(f,A,B)$;
  \item \inlinelean|zfun|: total functionality goals $\mathsf{IsFunc}(A,B,f)$.
\end{itemize}
All three tactics work similarly: they first search among local hypotheses for witnesses of the required properties, or apply standard theorems (e.g., composition of functions is a function) to reduce the goal to simpler subgoals.

They may then recursively call each other and backtrack as needed when application of a theorem fails.
In order to reduce search time and keep automation usable at runtime, some theorems are selected and tagged with relevant attributes---called \leaninl{zrel}, \leaninl{zpfun}, and \leaninl{zfun} for consistency with the tactic names---so that they can be found by the corresponding tactic.
This design is illustrated in \Cref{fig:tactics}.
This also allows all three tactics to be extended with new rules as needed.
When an automatic discharge fails, it falls back to the user for manual proof.
One can also pass explicit proofs to definitions and theorems instead of relying on automation, even when the tactics would succeed.

We show a few examples of such theorems (with proofs omitted) in \Cref{lst:automation-layer-thm}.
\begin{leanlisting}[title={Examples of theorems tagged for automation.}, label=lst:automation-layer-thm, float=t]
@[zrel] -- converse of a relation is a relation
theorem subset_prod_inv {R A B : ZFSet} (hR : R ⊆ A × B) : R⁻¹ ⊆ B × A
@[zpfun] -- identity is a partial function
theorem Id.IsPFunc {A : ZFSet} : (𝟙A).IsPFunc A A
@[zfun] -- composition of total functions is a total function
theorem IsFunc_of_composition_IsFunc {g f : ZFSet} {A B C : ZFSet}
  (hg : B.IsFunc C g) (hf : A.IsFunc B f) :
    A.IsFunc C (composition g f A B C)
\end{leanlisting}

Given this infrastructure, we then leverage Lean's \emph{automatic parameters} to pass default tactics in partial definitions depending on side-conditions, so that those side-conditions are automatically discharged when possible.
Automatic parameters are very similar to default parameters, but are specific to tactics: they try to discharge the goal using the provided default tactic if the argument is not supplied explicitly, and fail silently otherwise, leaving the user to provide a manual proof.
Furthermore, those side conditions often depend on parameters that are kept implicit (e.g., the sets $A$ and $B$ in $R \subseteq A \times B$); they are also inferred automatically by Lean.
This ultimately enables working with these definitions---most of the time---without worrying about side conditions or implicit parameters, which is the intended purpose of the \zflean{} framework.

\Cref{lst:automation-layer-thm} illustrates this already: the converse relation $R^{-1}$ in theorem \inlinelean|subset_prod_inv| is actually a relation between $B$ and $A$, which are implicit at this level, and relies on the side-condition $R \subseteq A \times B$, automatically discharged by \inlinelean|zrel|.
This principle will be further exemplified in \Cref{sec:zflean-case-study}.
\begin{leanlisting}[title={Examples of simplification rules for relations and functions.}, label=lst:simp-rules, float=t]
@[simp] theorem range_Id {A : ZFSet} : (𝟙A).Range = A
@[simp] theorem Image_empty {R A B : ZFSet} (hR : R ⊆ A.prod B) : R[$$∅$$] = ∅
@[simp] theorem Image_of_composition_inv_self_of_bijective {f A B X : ZFSet}
  {hf : A.IsFunc B f} (hf : f.IsBijective) (hX : X ⊆ A) : f⁻¹[f[X]] = X
\end{leanlisting}
We also tag relevant equality theorems with the \texttt{simp} attribute, so that they can be used by Lean's \inlinelean|simp| tactic for rewriting.
