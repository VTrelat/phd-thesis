\section{Use of AI}
\label{sec:intro-llm-use}

The rapid evolution described in \Cref{sec:intro-motivation} also affected the
practice of my research. I now use two LLM-based assistants as part of my
research workflow: ChatGPT~\cite{openai2022chatgpt} and
Claude~\cite{anthropic2023claude}. I introduced them into this workflow only in
the third year of the PhD, when their capabilities became sufficient for
sustained technical assistance. I use them for brainstorming, program
exploration, debugging and refactoring, Lean proof search, and the revision of
technical prose. I also use them as critics, asking them to identify unclear
explanations, propose alternative Lean proof scripts, and point to cases that
merit further investigation.

Although some design decisions were discussed with AI, mostly
GPT-5.4~\cite{openaiGPT54} and GPT-5.5~\cite{openaiGPT55}, I conceived and developed all
mathematical ideas, definitions, and proof arguments underlying my
contributions. For example, for the type-loosening approach of
\Cref{ch:loosening}, I asked the models to search the literature for related
ideas in other formalisms. I used these searches to check the novelty of my
contributions, verifying the references and comparing their definitions and
results with my own. The discussions also helped me find appropriate
terminology for the constructions I had developed.

For \beer{}, I designed the formalism, encoding, and correctness argument.
The proof proceeds by induction on the B syntax. I first proved as many
different kinds of case as possible myself, including basic values, variables,
pairs, addition, and set comprehension, whose proof had to account for bound
variables. I then asked LLM agents, mainly Claude Opus
4.6~\cite{anthropicClaudeOpus46}, 4.7~\cite{anthropicClaudeOpus47} and
4.8~\cite{anthropicClaudeOpus48}, to complete the remaining cases using these
proofs as models. A substantial part of the Lean correctness proofs was
generated in this way. The agents' role in the Lean development was limited
to proof engineering: adapting the established arguments, supplying auxiliary
lemmas, and carrying out the rewrites and other formal steps required by Lean.
This allowed me to complete proofs that I would otherwise have had to leave
unfinished within the time available. The
\href{https://github.com/VTrelat/BEer}{\beer{} repository} records Claude's
assistance through explicit co-author attribution in commit messages, allowing
the corresponding changes to be inspected.

For this manuscript and my papers, I write the text myself before asking AI
to check the grammar, suggest clearer or more concise formulations, and
identify passages that need further explanation. I then work through the
suggestions and revise the text, often over several iterations, checking
throughout that the wording preserves the intended mathematical meaning.
This workflow is now part of my daily routine.

I remain responsible for validating every output before incorporating it into
the thesis or its software artifacts. This includes checking formal developments
with Lean, exercising software changes through the relevant builds and tests,
verifying factual and bibliographic claims against primary sources, and
reviewing suggested statements and models against the intended B semantics.
Lean checks that a proof establishes the stated proposition relative to its
definitions and logical assumptions. Whether those definitions and statements
express the intended mathematics requires a separate review. The final
selection, interpretation, and presentation of all material are mine.

As part of an early generation of researchers using LLMs throughout their
work, I believe we have a responsibility to be honest and transparent about
that use and to develop practices that allow others to assess the resulting
research.
