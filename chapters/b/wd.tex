\subsection{Well-definedness}
\label{sec:b-wd}

Many B operators are \emph{partial}: they yield a meaningful value only on a strict subset
of their well-typed arguments. Function application \(\appB' f\,x\) presupposes that \(f\)
denotes a function and that \(x\) lies in its domain; cardinality \(\cardB{S}\) presupposes
that \(S\) is finite. Typing (\Cref{sec:b-typing}) constrains the \emph{shape} of these
expressions, but cannot express such conditions: they depend on the \emph{values} the
subterms denote, not on their syntax, and therefore lie at the semantic level.
The missing layer is \emph{well-definedness}---the
side conditions under which every partial operator in a term is applied within its domain.

\paragraph{Well-definedness in the B method.}
Partiality is a first-class concern of the proof-obligation generator. For each partial
operator occurring in a proof obligation, Atelier~B emits a separate \emph{well-definedness}
(WD) obligation---a side goal asserting that the operator is used within its domain---and
assumes that condition when discharging the main goal~\cite{abrial1996bbook}
(see~\Cref{sec:b-method}). A partial operator is read as a bounded Hilbert choice
\(\epsilon\,\var{x} \inB S \cdot \varphi\), left unspecified when no element of \(S\) satisfies
\(\varphi\); its WD condition is precisely that some element does. This keeps the goal logic
total, at the price of detaching the conditions from the operators that induce them: the
obligations are generated globally, and their correspondence with the partial operators of
the goal is left implicit.

\paragraph{A semantic account.}
The denotation \(\interpB{\cdot}\) developed above (\Cref{subsec:b-denote}) instead takes partiality
at face value: it is a genuine \emph{partial} function on terms, undefined exactly when a partial
operator is evaluated outside its domain. Well-definedness is then not an external catalogue of
obligations but a single predicate \(\regnotation{\WD}\) that collects, by structural recursion on
a term, the conditions under which all of its partial operators denote. Each condition is stated
directly against the denotations of subterms---the finiteness of a denoted set, the functionality
of a denoted relation---so that well-definedness is grounded in the very semantic objects it
constrains. Acting on the semantic domain \(\BDom\) of \Cref{def:b-dom} and the denotation
just introduced in \Cref{def:b-denote}, \(\WD\) is exactly the precondition under which that
denotation is total (\Cref{thm:b-denote-complete}).

\begin{definition}[Well-definedness predicate][B.PHOAS.WellDefined][B/Reasoning/WellDefined.html\#B.PHOAS.WellDefined]
  \label{def:b-welldefined}
  The predicate \(\WD : \mathsf{PHOAS.Term}\,\BDom \to \mathsf{Prop}\) is defined by structural
  recursion. It is purely propagational on the total fragment, and at the only two partial
  operators---application and cardinality---it additionally imposes
  the semantic side condition under which the operator denotes:
  \begin{fitalign}
    \WD(t)                          & \;\triangleq\; \top,\quad \text{ where \(t\) is an atom or a constant}, \\
    \WD\big(c(t_1, \dots, t_k)\big) & \;\triangleq\; \WD(t_1) \land \cdots \land \WD(t_k), \quad \text{ where \(c\) is a non-binder constructor}, \\
    \WD\big(\smallint_D P\big)       & \;\triangleq\; \WD(D) \land \forall \vec{\var{v}},\ \WD\big(P(\vec{\var{v}})\big), \quad \text{ where \(\smallint \in \left\{\lambdaBa, \forallBa, \bcompa{\cdot}{\cdot}\right\}\)}\\
    \WD\big(\appB' f\,x\big)        & \;\triangleq\; \WD(f) \land \WD(x) \land \IsPFunc\ F\ \interpZ{\tau}\ \interpZ{\sigma} \land X \in \Dom(F), \\
    \WD\big(\cardBa{S}\big)          & \;\triangleq\; \WD(S) \land \IsFinite\ S'.
  \end{fitalign}
  The first three clauses cover, respectively, an atom or type constant \(t\), a total non-binder
  constructor \(c\), and a binder \(\bcompa{D}{P}\)---the last applying identically to
  \(\lambdaB' D \cdot P\) and \(\forallB' D \cdot P\), and requiring the body \(P\) to be well-defined
  under every instantiation \(\vec{\var{v}}\) of its bound variables. In the two partial clauses the
  side conditions are stated against the subterm denotations: writing
  \(\interpB{f} = \denval{F}[\setB(\tau \prodB \sigma)]\), \(\interpB{x} = \denval{X}[\tau]\), and
  \(\interpB{S} = \denval{S'}[\setB\alpha]\), the value \(F\) must be a partial function from
  \(\interpZ{\tau}\) to \(\interpZ{\sigma}\) with \(X\) in its domain, and \(S'\) must be finite.
\end{definition}

The significance of well-definedness is fixed by the
typing-completeness theorem (\Cref{thm:b-denote-complete}): a well-typed term denotes a value of
its static type \emph{as soon as it is well-defined}. Indeed, \(\WD(t)\) supplies exactly the
witnesses---functionality and domain membership for application, and finiteness for
cardinality---that the partial clauses of \(\interpB{\cdot}\)
(\Cref{subsec:b-denote}) require in order to select their defined branch. Well-definedness is
thus the precise boundary between the partial denotation and the total restriction on which the
certified encoding of \Cref{ch:beer} relies. Defined here on the abstract syntax used for
semantic reasoning, the predicate transfers to the concrete first-order
syntax through abstraction, so that obligations stated over concrete terms inherit the same
discipline.

\begin{remark}[Well-definedness versus typing]
  This separation marks another divergence from the B-Book~\cite{abrial1996bbook}, which draws no sharp
  line between the two concerns: several of its well-definedness conditions fold in constraints
  that we classify as \emph{typing}. Here the two are kept apart. Typing (\Cref{sec:b-typing})
  governs the well-formedness of terms, whereas well-definedness asserts only bare semantic
  properties---finiteness, functionality, domain membership---and \emph{presupposes} typing rather
  than re-deriving it. This is why every theorem carries the two hypotheses side by side---\eg\Cref{thm:b-denote-complete}, \Cref{cor:b-denote-total}---a typing judgment together with
  \(\WD\). One could instead let \(\WD\) depend on a typing derivation and dispense with the
  separate premise; we deliberately avoid this, as it would thread derivations through the
  predicate and weigh down every statement, whereas the present form keeps \(\WD\) a lightweight,
  purely semantic side condition.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
  The term \(1 \inB \ZboolB\) is actually well-defined in our setting, as it contains no partial
  operator, yet it is \emph{not} well-typed, an integer being tested against a set of Booleans.
  Well-definedness admits it; typing rejects it. Unlike the B-Book, we regard this as the cleaner
  allocation of concerns: the defect is purely a matter of typing.
\end{example}
