\section{Satisfiability modulo theories}
\label{sec:ar-smt}

The Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) asks whether a propositional formula
admits a truth assignment under which it evaluates to true.
It is the prototypical NP-complete problem, yet this worst-case hardness is not
the whole story in practice: modern solvers based on \emph{conflict-driven
clause learning} (CDCL) decide industrial instances with millions of variables,
through a tight loop of Boolean propagation, heuristic decisions, and the
analysis of conflicts into learned clauses that prune the search
space~\cite{biere2021handbook}.

Propositional logic is, however, a coarse modeling language.
The proof obligations mentioned in \Cref{sec:b-method} speak of integers, sets,
functions, and relations, not merely of Boolean variables,
and reducing such constraints to propositional logic is either impossible---for
infinite domains---or prohibitively large.
\emph{Satisfiability modulo theories} (SMT) closes this gap by deciding the
satisfiability of first-order formulas \emph{with respect to} a fixed background
theory that constrains the interpretation of selected sorts and
symbols~\cite{barrett2021smt}.
It is the technology on which the encodings developed in the following chapters
ultimately rely.

\subsection{The SMT problem}
\label{subsec:smt-problem}

Formally~\cite{barrett2021smt}, one fixes a many-sorted first-order
\emph{signature} \(\Sigma\)---a collection of sorts together with the function
and predicate symbols and their arities---and a \emph{theory} \(T\), presented
either as a set of axioms or, more commonly, as a class of
\(\Sigma\)-\emph{structures}: the interpretations that equip each sort with a
non-empty domain and each symbol with an operation or relation of the matching
arity.
A formula \(\varphi\) is \emph{\(T\)-satisfiable} when at least one structure in
\(T\) satisfies it, and the SMT problem is to decide \(T\)-satisfiability for a
given fragment of \(\Sigma\)-formulas.
The benefit of this formulation is that the symbols of \(\Sigma\) are no longer
uninterpreted: the theory pins down the meaning of, for instance, \(+\),
\(\le\), or the array operations \(\mathsf{select}\) and \(\mathsf{store}\), so
that a solver may reason about them through a dedicated decision procedure rather
than from axioms alone.

A small, recurring collection of theories covers most practical needs:
equality with uninterpreted functions, linear arithmetic over the integers or
the reals, bit-vectors, arrays, algebraic datatypes, and strings, among others.
Each comes with a decision procedure for its quantifier-free fragment---and, for
several of them, for decidable quantified fragments as well; quantifiers outside
such a fragment are handled by the heuristic instantiation discussed below.
The task of a verification backend is then to decide the satisfiability of a
given obligation with respect to the relevant combination of these theories.

Most state-of-the-art solvers follow the \emph{lazy}, or \(\mathrm{DPLL}(T)\),
architecture~\cite{nieuwenhuis2006dpllt}.
A CDCL engine treats the atoms of the formula as opaque propositions and searches
for a propositional model; a dedicated \emph{theory solver} then checks whether
the conjunction of literals selected by that model is consistent in \(T\),
returning either a satisfying assignment or a \emph{theory lemma} that refines the
propositional search.

\begin{example}[A DPLL(\(T\)) refutation]
Consider the quantifier-free formula over equality and an uninterpreted
function~\(f\),
\[
  a = b \;\land\; b = c \;\land\; f(a) \neq f(c).
\]
Treating its three conjuncts as opaque propositions \(p\), \(q\), and \(r\) respectively,
the CDCL engine finds the propositional model \(p \land q \land r\) and hands the literals
\(\{a = b,\; b = c,\; f(a) \neq f(c)\}\) to the theory solver. Congruence
closure deduces \(a = c\) from \(a = b\) and \(b = c\), hence \(f(a) = f(c)\),
contradicting \(f(a) \neq f(c)\); it returns the theory lemma
\(\lnot p \lor \lnot q \lor \lnot r\). Added to the clause set, this lemma rules
out the only propositional model, so the formula is \emph{unsatisfiable}---a
verdict no purely Boolean reasoning could reach.
\end{example}

Reasoning over several theories at once is delegated to combination methods, the
foundational one being the \emph{Nelson--Oppen} procedure~\cite{nelson1979simplification}.
Quantifiers, which lie beyond the decidable core, are handled by heuristic
instantiation and are a frequent source of incompleteness.
Mature implementations of this architecture include cvc5~\cite{barbosa2022cvc5},
Z3~\cite{demoura2008z3}, and veriT~\cite{bouton2009verit}; the
SMT-LIB scripts produced in \Cref{ch:beer} are discharged by cvc5, whose support
for higher-order reasoning is exploited in the encodings of this thesis.

\subsection{Automated reasoning beyond SMT}
\label{subsec:automated-reasoning}

SMT sits within the broader field of \emph{automated reasoning}, and its
boundary with the rest of that field is one of method rather than of kind.
The term ``SMT'' names a \emph{problem}---deciding satisfiability modulo a
background theory---and not a particular algorithm.
The DPLL(\(T\)) design of \Cref{subsec:smt-problem} is the prevailing
architecture, but it does not fully constrain how the theory-reasoning component
reaches its verdict. At its core a theory solver is expected to be a
\emph{decision procedure} such as congruence closure for equality or the
simplex method for linear arithmetic. A general-purpose inference engine, which
is at best semi-decidable and does not in general satisfy the requirements for
theory combination, does not replace such a procedure; it is instead enlisted
alongside it to discharge the quantified goals that lie beyond the decidable
core, as we discuss next.

The most prominent general-purpose engine is \emph{saturation}.
Starting from the clausal form of a negated conjecture, a saturation procedure
repeatedly applies inference rules and accumulates the resulting clauses until
either the empty clause is derived---witnessing unsatisfiability---or no
non-redundant clause remains.
The inference system of reference is the \emph{superposition} calculus, a
refinement of resolution and \emph{paramodulation} that builds equality reasoning
into ordered rewriting and couples it with aggressive redundancy
elimination~\cite{bachmair1994superposition,nieuwenhuis2001paramodulation}.
Superposition reasons natively about quantifiers and equality---precisely the
ingredients that DPLL(\(T\)) handles only heuristically---and it also serves as a
decision procedure for several theories; for this reason it has been embedded in
SMT solvers as a theory and quantifier engine~\cite{demoura2008engineering}.

The leading saturation provers---Vampire~\cite{kovacs2013vampire}, the equational
prover E~\cite{schulz2013e}, and Zipperposition~\cite{bentkamp2023higherorder},
which extends superposition to higher-order logic---originate in the first-order
theorem-proving (ATP) tradition, whose canonical target is validity in quantified
logic with equality.
What separates the two traditions is thus emphasis rather than kind: historically
ATPs prioritize quantified first- and higher-order reasoning with equality,
whereas SMT solvers prioritize efficient, combinable decision procedures for rich
quantifier-free theories, and a sustained line of work continues to bring the two
closer together.

\subsection{Standards: SMT-LIB and TPTP}
\label{subsec:smt-lib-tptp}

The practical viability of both families rests on shared input standards, which
decouple the producers of reasoning problems from the solvers that discharge
them and thereby make large benchmark libraries and annual competitions
possible.
Two such standards are relevant here.
The \emph{TPTP} (Thousands of Problems for Theorem Provers) library and its
language serve the ATP community, spanning clause normal form, the typed
first-order form, and the typed higher-order form~\cite{sutcliffe2017tptp}, and
underpin the CASC competition.
The \emph{SMT-LIB} standard~\cite{smtlib2.7} plays the analogous role for SMT: it
fixes a many-sorted first-order logic, a catalog of theories and of named
\emph{logics}---such as \texttt{QF\_LIA} or \texttt{QF\_UFLRA}, which jointly
select a syntactic fragment and a theory combination---and a command language by
which a script declares sorts and symbols, asserts formulas, and queries the
solver.

\begin{example}[A minimal SMT-LIB script]
The following script, in the linear integer arithmetic logic, asks whether an
integer can be both positive and below \(1\); any SMT solver implementing
\texttt{QF\_LIA} should answer \texttt{unsat}.
\begin{smtlisting}
(set-logic QF_LIA)
(declare-const x Int)
(assert (and (> x 0) (< x 1)))
(check-sat)
\end{smtlisting}
\end{example}

A development of direct relevance is the recent evolution of the standard toward
higher-order logic.
Until version 2.6~\cite{smtlib2.6}, the SMT-LIB language was strictly many-sorted and
first-order: functions could be neither partially applied, nor passed as
arguments, nor quantified over.
Version 2.7, released in early 2025~\cite{smtlib2.7}, takes a deliberate first
step beyond this restriction.
While remaining backward compatible with 2.6, it adds a form of prenex
(rank-1) polymorphism in user-declared sorts and functions and, more
significantly, a theory of \emph{maps}---the \emph{higher-order core} fragment---that
models higher-order functions and so equips the language with a higher-order
syntax.
These additions are explicitly framed as a transition toward the forthcoming
version 3, whose base logic is to be a higher-order logic with polymorphic and
dependent types.

\begin{example}[A higher-order SMT-LIB script]
The new features let a function be passed as an argument and partially applied.
The script below declares a higher-order operator \smtinl{twice}, which applies
its function argument to a value twice, together with a binary symbol
\smtinl{f} whose partial application \smtinl{(f a)} has the function sort
\smtinl{(-> A A)}.
\begin{smtlisting}
(set-logic HO_ALL)
(declare-sort A 0)
(declare-const a A)
(define-fun twice ((g (-> A A)) (x A)) A (g (g x)))
(declare-const f (-> A A A))
(assert (not (= (twice (f a) a) (f a (f a a)))))
(check-sat)
\end{smtlisting}
Since \smtinl{(twice (f a) a)} unfolds to \smtinl{(f a (f a a))}, the assertion
is contradictory, and a solver like cvc5~\cite{barbosa2022cvc5} answers
\texttt{unsat}---an exchange that the strictly first-order language of
version~2.6 could not even express.

Note that here, \smtinl{f} can be declared either as
\begin{center}
  \vspace*{-.5\baselineskip}
  \smtinl{declare-const f (-> A A A)},
  or as \smtinl{declare-fun f (A A) A},
  \vspace*{-.5\baselineskip}
\end{center}
the two forms being accepted by the standard as equivalent.  
\end{example}
