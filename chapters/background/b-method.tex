\section{The B Method}
\label{sec:b-method-atelier-b}
\label{sec:b-method}

The B Method is a state-based formal method for the development of correct-by-construction software systems.
Its central idea is to make the mathematical model, the implementation steps, and the proof obligations part of the same development process.
Instead of treating a program as an artifact that is validated only after it has been written, B starts from an abstract machine, equips it with an invariant, and then checks each operation against that invariant.
Refinement then turns the abstract model into progressively more concrete machines while preserving the properties already established at the abstract level.

The classical presentation of B is due to Abrial's \emph{B-Book}~\cite{abrial1996bbook}; Schneider's introduction~\cite{schneider2001bmethod} gives a more tutorial account of the same development discipline.
In this thesis, B is used less as a full software engineering method than as a source of proof obligations whose mathematical content is rich enough to stress SMT encodings.
This distinction matters: the work formalized in BEer does not attempt to mechanize the entire B development method, including every clause of the machine language and the complete refinement calculus.
It focuses on the logical core that appears in Atelier B proof obligations: typed expressions, predicates, sets, products, partial functions, and the connectives needed to state proof obligations.

\subsection{Machines, invariants, and proof obligations}

A B development is organized around \emph{machines}.
A machine declares state variables, constants, sets, invariants, properties, and operations.
The invariant is the central specification device: it defines the states that are considered valid.
Each operation is required to preserve this invariant, and the initialization is required to establish it.
The proof obligations generated by a B tool are the logical conditions that justify those requirements.

At a high level, an invariant-preservation obligation has the following form:
\[
  I(v) \andB P(v, x) \;\Rightarrow\; I(v')
\]
where \(v\) denotes the current state, \(x\) denotes operation parameters, \(P\) denotes the operation predicate or substitution semantics, and \(v'\) denotes the state after the operation.
The actual obligations generated by industrial B tools are more structured: they include typing hypotheses, definitions, well-definedness hypotheses, invariant hypotheses, assertion hypotheses, and goals.
The BEer environment mirrors this organization by storing proof obligations as lists of definitions, hypotheses, and simple goals.
The shape is intentionally close to the proof-obligation files read from Atelier B, because the encoder must preserve the structure that matters for translation to SMT.

\subsection{Refinement}

Refinement is the other major pillar of the B Method.
An abstract machine may use mathematical objects that are convenient for specification but unsuitable for execution.
A refinement replaces these objects and operations by more concrete ones, while generating proof obligations that show the refinement simulates the abstract behavior.
If the abstract invariant and properties have been proved, and if every refinement step is discharged, the final implementation inherits the established correctness properties.

The present thesis does not formalize refinement.
However, refinement explains why B proof obligations are a valuable benchmark for SMT-oriented reasoning.
They mix first-order arithmetic, finite and infinite sets, products, relations, partial functions, and quantified predicates.
Even when a proof obligation comes from a concrete implementation step, it is stated in a mathematical language rather than in the executable target language.
An SMT encoder for B must therefore respect the mathematical reading of B expressions, not merely a syntactic approximation of them.

\subsection{Why the B core matters here}

The objective of BEer is to translate B proof obligations to SMT-LIB while retaining a mechanically checked account of the translation.
This requires a precise source language.
For this thesis, the relevant source is a core B expression language with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item integers and booleans;
  \item ordered pairs, represented in B notation by maplets;
  \item sets, powersets, Cartesian products, union, intersection, and cardinality;
  \item predicates, equality, membership, ordering, conjunction, and negation;
  \item binders for universal quantification, set comprehension, and lambda abstraction;
  \item partial functions and function application.
\end{itemize}

This core is small compared with the full B language, but it contains the constructs that drive the encoding problems studied later.
In particular, sets and partial functions are not peripheral features: in B, they are the primary way to describe data structures and operation effects.
