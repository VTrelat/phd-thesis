# PhD Manuscript Outline

**Working title:** *Certified Encoding of B Proof Obligations into SMT-LIB 2.7*

This outline is organised as a conventional PhD manuscript. Each chapter has a distinct role in the argument. BEer remains the central contribution; ZFLean and BARReL each receive an autonomous chapter; and type loosening is introduced as its own technical contribution between the formal source/target languages and the BEer encoder.

---

## Overall structure

1. **Introduction**
2. **Background and Technical Preliminaries**
3. **ZFLean: Set-Level Mathematics in Lean**
4. **Formalisation of B Proof Obligations**
5. **Formalisation of SMT-LIB 2.7**
6. **Type Loosening: From Functions to Relations**
7. **BEer: Certified Encoding and Lean Implementation**
8. **BARReL: A Lean Backend for Atelier B**
9. **Evaluation**
10. **Related Work**
11. **Conclusion and Perspectives**

## Chapter 1 — Introduction

The introduction motivates the problem, states the thesis, identifies the contributions, and explains the organisation of the manuscript. It should avoid technical definitions and detailed encoding rules.

### 1.1 Motivation

Introduce the context of industrial formal methods and proof-obligation generation. Explain why automatic proof is essential in a method such as B, and why external solvers are attractive but raise a trust problem.

### 1.2 Research problem

State the central problem of the thesis:

> How can proof obligations generated by Atelier B be translated into SMT-LIB 2.7 in a way that is executable, faithful to the semantics of B, and amenable to mechanised justification?

Present the semantic gap between B and SMT-LIB at a high level. The detailed issues of typing, binding, partiality, functions, relations, and solver interaction should be announced here, then developed in later chapters.

### 1.3 Thesis statement

Formulate the main claim of the manuscript. For example:

> This thesis shows that a substantial fragment of Atelier B proof obligations can be encoded into SMT-LIB 2.7 by a Lean implementation whose core transformation is specified, structured by a type-loosening mechanism, and semantically justified.

### 1.4 Contributions

Present the contributions in decreasing order of importance.

1. **BEer**, the main contribution: a certified encoder from Atelier B proof obligations to SMT-LIB 2.7.
2. **Type loosening**, a central encoding mechanism allowing B functions to be viewed as relations when required by relational operators.
3. **ZFLean**, a Lean framework for set-level mathematical development, used to support the semantics of B-style sets, relations, and functions.
4. **BARReL**, a Lean backend for Atelier B proof obligations, developed as a companion backend.

The text should make clear that BEer is the central artefact, while type loosening is one of the main technical ideas enabling the encoder.

### 1.5 Methodology

Describe the general methodology: define formal source and target languages, give them typing rules and semantics, define the type-loosening interface, implement and prove the encoding in Lean, and evaluate the resulting artefact on proof obligations.

### 1.6 Manuscript overview

Give a concise roadmap of the remaining chapters.

## Chapter 2 — Background and Technical Preliminaries

This chapter provides the necessary background for a reader who knows formal methods or theorem proving, but not necessarily all of B, SMT-LIB, ZFC, Lean, or PHOAS. It should remain expository.

### 2.1 The B Method and Atelier B

Introduce B machines, state variables, invariants, operations, refinement, and proof obligations. Explain the role of Atelier B and the generation of POG files. Present B as a formal method whose proof obligations are expressed in a rich set-theoretic language.

### 2.2 Automated reasoning and SMT solving

Explain the SMT problem, the relationship between validity and unsatisfiability, the role of background theories, and the practical behaviour of solvers on quantified formulas. Solver answers such as `sat`, `unsat`, and `unknown` should be discussed in the prose, not promoted to separate sections.

### 2.3 SMT-LIB as a solver interface language

Present SMT-LIB scripts, declarations, assertions, sorts, terms, and commands. Introduce the features of SMT-LIB 2.7 that matter for the thesis, especially where they influence the BEer target language.

### 2.4 Set theory and B mathematics

Recall the set-theoretic notions used throughout the manuscript: membership, extensionality, ordered pairs, Cartesian products, relations, functions, powersets, and integer constructions. The goal is not to present ZFC exhaustively, but to prepare the reader for B’s mathematical universe and for ZFLean.

### 2.5 Lean

Introduce Lean as both a proof assistant and an executable programming language. Explain the aspects needed later: inductive datatypes, recursive functions, propositions as types, theorem statements, tactics, and metaprogramming where relevant.

### 2.6 Syntax with binders

Explain the general problem of representing binders: capture, substitution, alpha-equivalence, and scope. Compare named representations, de Bruijn indices, HOAS, and PHOAS at a conceptual level, then indicate why PHOAS is useful in the formalisation.

## Chapter 3 — ZFLean: Set-Level Mathematics in Lean

This chapter presents ZFLean as an autonomous contribution. Its role is to provide the set-level mathematical infrastructure needed by the later formalisation of B and by the correctness proof of BEer.

### 3.1 Motivation

Explain why B proof obligations naturally require set-level reasoning. Emphasise the mismatch between B-style mathematics, where functions are relations and relations are sets of pairs, and Lean’s ordinary typed mathematical style.

### 3.2 Design of ZFLean

Present the core design choices: a set-level universe, explicit membership, extensionality principles, typed interfaces over set-level objects, and definitions chosen to support rewriting and automation.

### 3.3 Basic set constructions

Define and discuss the constructions needed later: empty set, singleton, pair set, union, intersection, set difference, powerset, and separation-like constructions.

### 3.4 Ordered pairs and products

Present the encoding of ordered pairs, projections, Cartesian products, product membership, and the main extensionality lemmas for products.

### 3.5 Relations

Develop relations as sets of ordered pairs. Include domain, range, converse, composition, identity relation, relational image, restrictions, and override. State the principal algebraic properties used later in the thesis.

### 3.6 Functions

Develop functions as functional relations. Include partial functions, total functions, function application, function spaces, injections, surjections, bijections, and function extensionality.

### 3.7 Arithmetic and finite constructions

Present the set-level treatment of natural numbers, integers, intervals, finite sets, and cardinality if it is part of the formalisation.

### 3.8 Proof automation

Describe the rewriting principles, extensionality lemmas, and simplification procedures that make ZFLean practical for larger developments.

### 3.9 Use of ZFLean in the thesis

Explain precisely how ZFLean supports the B semantics, the semantics of relations and functions, and the semantic preservation arguments for the encoder.

### 3.10 Limitations and perspectives

Discuss the current limitations of ZFLean and possible extensions after the thesis.

This chapter defines the source language of BEer. It should focus on proof-obligation B, not on the full B language as written by users.

### 4.1 Scope of the formalisation

Specify which fragment of B proof obligations is formalised, how it relates to Atelier B POG files, and which constructs are intentionally left outside the formal core.

### 4.2 Concrete and parsed syntax

Present the relevant concrete B syntax and the structure of POG files. Explain how proof obligations, hypotheses, goals, type information, and definitions are represented at input level.

### 4.3 Abstract syntax

Define the abstract syntax used in the formalisation: type expressions, expressions, predicates, definitions, contexts, and proof obligations. Discuss the treatment of variables and binders.

### 4.4 Typing rules

Give the typing judgments for expressions and predicates. Cover sets, products, relations, functions, arithmetic expressions, quantified predicates, and definitions.

### 4.5 Well-definedness

Explain how partial operators are treated. Define the well-definedness judgments for expressions and predicates, and relate them to the proof obligations produced by Atelier B.

### 4.6 Semantics

Define semantic environments, interpretation of types, denotation of expressions, satisfaction of predicates, and validity of proof obligations. This section should use ZFLean where appropriate.

### 4.7 Design choices

Justify the main formalisation choices: working at proof-obligation level, using set-theoretic semantics, making partiality explicit, and choosing a binder representation suitable for mechanised proofs.

## Chapter 6 — Type Loosening: From Functions to Relations

This chapter introduces type loosening as a central technical contribution. It explains how BEer reconciles B’s set-theoretic treatment of functions with target representations that distinguish functional and relational views.

### 6.1 Motivation

Explain the core difficulty: in B, functions are also relations and sets of ordered pairs. A term may therefore be used as a function in one context and as a relation in another. The encoder needs a principled way to move from a precise functional view to a looser relational view when required.

Typical motivating examples include function application, domain, range, relational image, converse, composition, membership of pairs in functions, and equality between expressions used at different levels of precision.

### 6.2 Function and relation views in B

Describe the hierarchy of B mathematical objects relevant to the chapter: sets, relations, partial functions, total functions, injections, surjections, bijections, and finite function spaces. Emphasise that these are not disjoint concepts in B; they form a refinement structure.

### 6.3 Why rigid type translation is insufficient

Explain why assigning each B type a single fixed SMT representation is too restrictive. A function represented as a target map is convenient for application, but a relational operator expects a set of pairs. Conversely, representing everything as a relation can obscure functional structure and harm automation.

### 6.4 The loosening relation on types

Define the formal relation between precise and looser B types. The central rule is that a function type may be viewed as the corresponding relation type. Include the reflexive cases and the structural cases for sets, products, relations, and function-space constructors as needed.

A typical notation is:

\[
\tau \preceq \tau'
\]

meaning that a value of type \(\tau\) may be used at the looser type \(\tau'\).

### 6.5 Semantic coercions

Define the semantic operation associated with type loosening. For a function, this operation forgets the functional structure and keeps the graph as a relation. More generally, it maps a denotation at a precise type to a denotation at the requested looser type.

The central semantic property should express that loosening preserves the underlying B object up to the intended coercion.

### 6.6 Loosening-guided translation

Introduce the translation judgment that makes expected types explicit. Instead of translating an expression in isolation, the encoder translates it under an expected view. This explains where function-to-relation conversions are inserted.

For example, function application requests a functional view, whereas domain, range, converse, composition, inclusion, and pair membership request relational or set-of-pairs views.

### 6.7 Common loose views

Explain how the encoder handles constructs involving multiple expressions, such as equality, inclusion, membership, and conditional expressions. These may require computing a common type at which the compared or combined expressions can be translated.

### 6.8 Algorithmic computation

Describe the algorithm implemented by BEer for computing loosening coercions and common loose views. State its expected properties: determinism, termination, soundness with respect to the formal loosening relation, and failure behaviour when no admissible common view exists.

### 6.9 Correctness of type loosening

State the main correctness theorem for the chapter. Informally:

> Translating an expression under a looser expected type preserves its B denotation, up to the semantic coercion associated with the loosening relation.

This theorem should later be used as a lemma in the global soundness proof of BEer.

### 6.10 Design consequences and limitations

Explain what the mechanism buys: the encoder can keep functional structure where useful and recover relational structure where required by B. Discuss limitations such as ambiguous common views, possible loss of solver performance after relationalisation, interaction with quantifiers, and unsupported operators.

## Chapter 7 — BEer: Certified Encoding and Lean Implementation

This chapter presents BEer as the main artefact of the thesis. It combines the semantic and implementation views while keeping them clearly separated within the chapter.

### 7.1 Overview

State the purpose of BEer and present the end-to-end pipeline:

\[
\texttt{POG} \to \text{B proof obligation} \to \text{typed internal representation} \to \text{SMT-LIB script}.
\]

Explain that the encoding relies on the source formalisation of Chapter 4, the target formalisation of Chapter 5, and the type-loosening mechanism of Chapter 6.

### 7.2 Semantic encoding of proof obligations

Define the translation of a B proof obligation into an SMT-LIB satisfiability problem. Explain the validity-to-unsatisfiability reduction: hypotheses are asserted, the goal is negated, and the solver is asked to prove unsatisfiability.

### 7.3 Translation of types, expressions, and predicates

Present the main encoding rules. Organise them by mathematical family: base values, sets, products, relations, functions, arithmetic, logical connectives, quantifiers, and well-definedness constraints. Refer back to Chapter 6 whenever a function is translated through a looser relational view.

### 7.4 SMT-LIB script generation

Explain how declarations, definitions, assertions, prelude material, and final commands are assembled into a complete SMT-LIB script.

### 7.5 Lean implementation architecture

Present the implementation structure: POG parser, raw syntax, typed internal representation, contexts, encoding functions, SMT-LIB syntax, pretty-printer, command-line interface, and solver interface.

### 7.6 Handling of binders, names, and contexts

Describe how BEer represents bound variables, manages contexts, generates fresh names, preserves source-level distinctions when necessary, and avoids capture.

### 7.7 Mechanised correctness

State the main formal results: type preservation, well-formedness of generated SMT-LIB, correctness of type loosening as used by the encoder, semantic preservation of expressions and predicates, and soundness of proof-obligation translation.

The main theorem should have the following informal form:

\[
\operatorname{Unsat}(\operatorname{BEer}(PO))
\Rightarrow
\operatorname{Valid}_B(PO).
\]

The assumptions of this theorem should be stated explicitly.

### 7.8 Trusted computing base

Identify the trusted components: Lean kernel and runtime, XML parsing if not verified, pretty-printing if not verified, the SMT solver, the solver’s SMT-LIB parser, and the intended interpretation of prelude axioms.

### 7.9 Limitations

Discuss unsupported B constructs, unverified implementation components, solver-dependent behaviour, performance bottlenecks, and the current status of mechanisation.

## Chapter 8 — BARReL: A Lean Backend for Atelier B

This chapter presents BARReL as a companion contribution. It should be shorter than the BEer chapter but still autonomous.

### 8.1 Motivation

Explain why one may want to translate Atelier B proof obligations directly to Lean rather than to SMT-LIB. Position BARReL as complementary to BEer: less solver-oriented, more proof-assistant-oriented.

### 8.2 Architecture

Present the BARReL pipeline from POG input to generated Lean goals. Explain the main layers: parsing, internal representation, translation to Lean expressions, generated declarations, and tactic-based proof attempts.

### 8.3 Embedding of B in Lean

Describe how B sets, relations, functions, arithmetic, predicates, quantifiers, and definitions are represented in Lean.

### 8.4 Lean metaprogramming

Discuss expression construction, elaboration issues, generated declarations, tactic integration, and error reporting.

### 8.5 Example

Give one complete example: an input proof obligation, the generated Lean statement, and the corresponding proof script or tactic proof. Compare briefly with what BEer would generate for the same obligation.

### 8.6 Discussion

Analyse the advantages and limitations of BARReL. Explain what it shows about backend design for Atelier B and how it could converge with or complement BEer in future work.

## Chapter 9 — Evaluation

This chapter evaluates the artefacts of the thesis, with BEer as the primary object of evaluation.

### 9.1 Evaluation goals

State the questions addressed by the evaluation: language coverage, correctness coverage, automation, performance, usability, and reproducibility.

### 9.2 Benchmark corpus

Describe the source of the proof obligations, the classification of examples, and the criteria for inclusion or exclusion.

### 9.3 Experimental setup

Report the Lean version, BEer version, solver versions, hardware, timeout policy, and reproducibility artefacts.

### 9.4 BEer results

Present translation success rate, generated SMT-LIB size, encoding time, solver success rate, and failure analysis. Separate failures due to unsupported input, encoding limitations, solver incompleteness, and timeouts.

### 9.5 Impact of type loosening

Evaluate the practical effect of the type-loosening mechanism. Discuss examples where function-to-relation conversion is necessary, cases where it improves expressiveness, and cases where it harms or complicates solver automation.

### 9.6 ZFLean results

Evaluate ZFLean as proof infrastructure: reusable definitions, reusable lemmas, proof-engineering benefits, and its role in the correctness proof.

### 9.7 BARReL results

Report selected results for BARReL: translated obligations, generated Lean goals, tactic success rate where relevant, and qualitative comparison with BEer.

### 9.8 Threats to validity

Discuss benchmark representativeness, solver dependence, trusted components, partial formal coverage, and implementation-specific assumptions.

### 10.2 SMT encodings of B proof obligations

### 10.3 SMT-LIB and higher-order SMT

Position the target language choices with respect to the evolution of SMT-LIB and higher-order SMT support.

### 10.4 Certified translators and verified compilers

Relate BEer to semantic preservation, compiler correctness, proof-producing translations, and verified solver interfaces.

### 10.5 Set theory in proof assistants

Compare ZFLean with other set-theoretic developments and with typed encodings of set-level mathematics.

### 10.6 Lean-based verification tools

Position both BEer and BARReL within the ecosystem of Lean-based verification and metaprogramming tools.

### 10.7 Syntax with binders

Compare the binder representation used in the thesis with named, de Bruijn, locally nameless, HOAS, and PHOAS approaches.

### 10.8 Summary of positioning

Conclude with a synthetic comparison showing what is specific to BEer, to type loosening, to ZFLean, and to BARReL.

## Chapter 11 — Conclusion and Perspectives

This chapter closes the manuscript by restating the results and identifying future work.

### 11.1 Summary

Summarise the central line of the thesis: B proof obligations are formalised, their target SMT-LIB fragment is formalised, the type mismatch is handled through type loosening, and BEer implements a semantically justified encoding in Lean.

### 11.2 Main achievements

Discuss the achievements contribution by contribution: BEer, type loosening, ZFLean, and BARReL.

### 11.3 Limitations

Discuss language coverage, solver trust, parser and pretty-printer trust, incomplete automation, performance limitations, and remaining mechanisation work.

### 11.4 Future work

Present future directions: larger B coverage, proof certificates from SMT solvers, countermodel reconstruction, richer use of type loosening, stronger integration between BEer and BARReL, and further development of ZFLean.
